Tradition Six helps Alcoholics Anonymous groups stay focused on recovery rather than becoming entangled with outside organizations, business interests or public causes. In simple terms, an AA group can cooperate with others, but it should not endorse, finance or attach the AA name to an outside enterprise.

This does not mean AA must isolate itself from treatment providers, community centers, healthcare professionals or other recovery resources. It means maintaining clear boundaries so that questions of money, ownership, influence and reputation do not distract the group from helping people seeking sobriety.

For newcomers, Tradition Six can explain why an AA meeting may rent space from a religious organization or community facility without being part of that organization. It also explains why a group generally avoids recommending a particular treatment center, therapist, business or political campaign in the name of AA.

## What Does AA Tradition Six Say?

The short form of Tradition Six states:

> “An A.A. group ought never endorse, finance, or lend the A.A. name to any related facility or outside enterprise, lest problems of money, property, and prestige divert us from our primary purpose.”

Several phrases are important:

- **Endorse** means publicly approving, recommending or appearing to guarantee an outside organization, product or cause.
- **Finance** means using AA group funds to support an outside enterprise.
- **Lend the AA name** means allowing an outside project to present itself as officially connected with, approved by or operated by Alcoholics Anonymous.
- **Outside enterprise** can include a business, charity, treatment facility, club, campaign, publication or other organization that is not part of AA’s service structure.
- **Primary purpose** refers to AA’s central focus on carrying its recovery message to people with alcohol problems.

If you are unfamiliar with AA’s basic structure, [what Alcoholics Anonymous is and how it works](/blog/what-is-alcoholics-anonymous-and-how-does-it-work) provides a newcomer-friendly overview of meetings, membership, sponsorship and the Twelve Steps.

## Why Tradition Six Matters

The central concern behind Tradition Six is distraction. Even a well-intentioned outside project can create disagreements about money, authority, property and reputation.

### Money can influence decisions

Suppose an AA group invests in a recovery-related business or gives group contributions to an outside organization. Members may then disagree about how money is spent, who controls it or whether the organization is producing results.

Financial involvement can also make it harder for the group to speak or act independently. Tradition Six reduces this risk by keeping group funds focused on appropriate AA expenses and services. This principle works alongside [Tradition Seven’s approach to self-support and financial independence](/blog/understanding-tradition-seven).

### Property can shift attention away from recovery

Managing substantial property or an outside facility creates practical responsibilities. People must make decisions about ownership, insurance, maintenance, employment and legal obligations.

Those matters may be necessary for the organization that owns the property, but they can consume the time and energy of an AA group. Tradition Six encourages the group to avoid taking on responsibilities that could overwhelm its recovery purpose.

### Prestige can weaken equality

Prestige involves status, influence or public recognition. If an AA group becomes closely identified with a successful organization, prominent person or popular cause, members may begin protecting that relationship or reputation.

This can create informal power structures and make some voices seem more important than others. By declining outside endorsements, AA groups protect the principle that members meet as equals around a shared recovery purpose.

## Cooperation Is Not the Same as Affiliation

Tradition Six does not prohibit practical cooperation. AA meetings often take place in hospitals, religious buildings, treatment facilities, community centers and other rented or donated spaces. Meeting information may also be shared with healthcare professionals or local support services.

The important distinction is between **cooperation** and **affiliation**:

- Cooperation allows people and organizations to exchange useful information or make space available.
- Affiliation suggests ownership, sponsorship, endorsement or formal partnership.

For example, a community center may rent a room to an AA group. That does not make the center an AA organization, and it does not mean AA endorses every activity held there.

Similarly, a treatment facility might provide patients with publicly available AA meeting information. This does not mean AA operates, certifies or recommends that facility.

Clear wording matters. A meeting announcement can identify a location without implying that the host organization sponsors the meeting. Local AA service resources may help groups address questions about listings, public information and relationships with community organizations.

## Practical Examples of Tradition Six

Tradition Six becomes easier to understand when applied to ordinary group decisions.

### Recommending a treatment provider

Members are free to describe their personal experiences with treatment, counseling or healthcare. However, an AA group formally naming one provider as “AA approved” would suggest an endorsement that AA is not qualified to give.

A group may instead make general resource information available while clearly separating it from AA. Individual members can also encourage someone to consult a qualified healthcare professional without claiming that AA guarantees a particular provider.

### Supporting a charity with group funds

A charity may do valuable work related to homelessness, mental health, families or substance use. Tradition Six still suggests that an AA group should not direct group contributions to that outside cause.

Individual AA members remain free to donate privately. The boundary concerns what the AA group does in AA’s name, not members’ personal charitable choices.

### Promoting a recovery product or service

A member may write a book, run a sober-living residence, provide therapy or own a recovery-related business. Those activities do not automatically violate an AA tradition because traditions guide AA groups and AA as a whole rather than controlling members’ private lives.

The problem arises when the AA name, meeting platform or group contact list is used to promote the service. Members should not feel that participation in AA comes with pressure to buy something or use a particular provider.

### Joining a public campaign

An AA group may care deeply about alcohol-related harm, healthcare access or other community concerns. However, formally joining a political or advocacy campaign can imply that AA supports the campaign’s full position.

Tradition Six discourages this kind of attachment, while Tradition Ten separately addresses AA’s avoidance of opinions on outside issues. The two are related but not identical. You can explore that distinction in this guide to [AA Tradition Ten and public controversy](/blog/understanding-tradition-ten).

### Using social media partnerships

Online relationships can create subtle forms of endorsement. Tagging a business as a “trusted partner,” accepting sponsored content or repeatedly promoting one treatment service through a group account may imply affiliation.

Groups can keep online communication focused on accurate meeting information and AA-related service. Any links to outside resources should be presented carefully so that users are not misled about endorsement.

## How an AA Group Can Apply Tradition Six

Questions about Tradition Six are usually best considered through an informed group conscience. In AA, a group conscience is the process by which members discuss an issue and seek a decision based on AA principles rather than one person’s authority.

Before agreeing to an outside relationship, members might ask:

1. **Does this arrangement help us carry the AA message, or could it distract us?**
2. **Could a newcomer reasonably think AA endorses this organization?**
3. **Are group funds being used for an outside purpose?**
4. **Are we taking responsibility for property, staff or services beyond the group’s role?**
5. **Could personal business interests affect the decision?**
6. **Is the AA name or meeting contact list being used to promote something?**
7. **Can we cooperate without creating a formal affiliation?**
8. **Would a simple disclaimer or clearer wording prevent confusion?**

Groups do not need to approach every outside contact with suspicion. The aim is clarity, not isolation. Pausing to ask these questions can prevent misunderstandings and protect trust.

## Does Tradition Six Control Individual AA Members?

AA traditions primarily guide groups and AA as a whole. They are not laws governing every private decision made by a member.

An individual may work for a treatment provider, support a charity, participate in politics, follow a religion or recommend a professional based on personal experience. The key is not to suggest that a private opinion represents Alcoholics Anonymous.

It can help to say, “This is my personal experience,” rather than, “AA recommends this.” Members with professional or financial interests should also be transparent where those interests could affect a group discussion.

Healthy boundaries protect both the individual and the meeting. They allow members to have full lives outside AA without turning the fellowship into a platform for business, professional or ideological interests.

## What Tradition Six Means for Newcomers

For someone attending an AA meeting for the first time, Tradition Six offers reassurance. The meeting should not be a sales opportunity, recruitment channel or gateway to a favored outside service.

AA is one peer-support route among several approaches to recovery. It does not replace medical care, mental health treatment or professional support. A responsible meeting can cooperate with the wider community while remaining clear about what AA does and does not provide.

## Conclusion

Tradition Six protects AA’s focus by separating the fellowship from outside businesses, facilities, causes and financial interests. It does not reject cooperation. Instead, it asks groups to cooperate without endorsement or affiliation.

By maintaining these boundaries, AA groups can reduce conflict over money, property and prestige—and keep their attention on offering peer support and a path toward recovery.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can an AA meeting be held in a church, hospital or treatment center?

Yes. An AA group can use or rent space in an outside facility without becoming affiliated with it. Clear meeting listings and financial arrangements help prevent the impression that either organization endorses the other.

### Can an AA group recommend a therapist or treatment program?

Tradition Six cautions against group endorsement of a particular outside provider. Members may share personal experiences, and meetings may provide general resource information, but AA should not present a provider as officially approved or guaranteed.

### Can an AA member own a recovery-related business?

Yes. Tradition Six does not prohibit members from working in healthcare, operating businesses or participating in outside organizations. The member should avoid using the AA name, meeting access or service position to promote a private enterprise.

### Is accepting free meeting space an outside contribution?

The details may depend on the arrangement and the informed group conscience. Groups often consider both Tradition Six and Tradition Seven when discussing free or reduced-cost space. The central questions are whether the arrangement threatens independence, creates obligations or implies endorsement. Local AA service experience may help a group reach a careful decision.
