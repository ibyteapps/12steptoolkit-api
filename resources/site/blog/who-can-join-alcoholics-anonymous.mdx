If you want to stop drinking, you can consider joining Alcoholics Anonymous. You do not need a medical diagnosis, a referral, a particular background or a history of severe consequences. You also do not have to wait until you have “hit rock bottom”.

AA’s stated membership requirement is simple: **a desire to stop drinking**. That desire may be firm, tentative or newly forming. You can be uncertain about whether AA is right for you and still attend an appropriate meeting to find out more.

Alcoholics Anonymous is a peer-support fellowship rather than a medical service. Its members share their experience of alcohol problems and recovery, often using the Twelve Steps as a framework. AA is one possible source of support, not the only route to recovery, and it can be used alongside professional healthcare or other recovery approaches.

## What is the requirement for joining AA?

Alcoholics Anonymous does not require prospective members to complete an application, obtain a referral or prove that they have an alcohol problem. There are no formal educational, financial, religious or social qualifications.

The key requirement is that you want to stop drinking. In practical terms, this means you might consider AA if:

- you have tried to control or stop your drinking and found it difficult;
- alcohol is affecting your health, relationships, work or peace of mind;
- you can stop for a while but struggle to remain stopped;
- you are worried about what may happen if your drinking continues;
- you are already sober and want support to maintain that change;
- you simply know that you no longer want alcohol to be part of your life.

You do not have to identify with every story you hear in a meeting. People arrive at AA with very different experiences. Some have lost jobs, relationships or homes, while others seek support before those consequences occur. If you are waiting for a dramatic crisis before asking for help, it may be useful to explore why [you do not have to hit rock bottom to stop drinking](/blog/can-you-stop-drinking-without-hitting-rock-bottom).

## Do I have to call myself an alcoholic?

No one else can make that decision for you. “Alcoholic” is a word commonly used by AA members to describe their own relationship with alcohol, but AA is not a diagnostic service.

At some meetings, members introduce themselves by name and may describe themselves as alcoholic. A newcomer can usually introduce themselves simply by giving their first name, or may choose only to listen. You do not need to adopt language that you do not yet understand or feel comfortable using.

If you are unsure how serious your drinking has become, you can reflect on patterns such as loss of control, secrecy, blackouts, increasing tolerance, withdrawal symptoms or repeated unsuccessful attempts to cut down. Our guide to [recognising signs of a drinking problem](/blog/how-do-i-know-if-i-have-a-drinking-problem) offers questions to consider without trying to diagnose you.

A GP, alcohol service or other qualified healthcare professional can assess your health and discuss treatment options. AA members may share personal experience, but they do not provide a clinical diagnosis.

## Who can attend an AA meeting?

The answer depends partly on whether the meeting is described as **open** or **closed**.

### Closed AA meetings

Closed meetings are intended for people who have a desire to stop drinking. This includes newcomers who are exploring AA and people who have been sober for many years.

“Closed” does not mean that you need an invitation or established membership. It describes who the meeting is for. If you want to stop drinking, you can generally attend a closed meeting even if it is your first one.

### Open AA meetings

Open meetings may also be attended by people who are interested in learning about Alcoholics Anonymous. Depending on the meeting, this can include relatives, friends, healthcare professionals, students or other observers.

Open meetings can be useful if you want a supportive person to accompany you. However, visitors are normally expected to respect the meeting’s anonymity and avoid sharing identifiable details about attendees outside the room.

Meeting formats and local arrangements vary, so check the listing or contact the local AA service if you are uncertain whether a particular meeting is suitable.

## Can young people join Alcoholics Anonymous?

Alcohol problems do not affect only older adults. A younger person who wants to stop drinking may seek help through AA, and some areas have meetings or resources particularly welcoming to younger members.

Age-related arrangements can vary, especially for anyone under 18. A young person can contact the relevant local AA service to ask what meetings are available and whether any safeguarding arrangements apply. Support may also be available through a GP, school or college wellbeing service, local alcohol service or trusted adult.

If a young person may be physically dependent on alcohol, professional medical advice is particularly important. Abruptly stopping after sustained heavy drinking can be dangerous for some people.

## Do I need to be religious to join AA?

No. AA is described as spiritual rather than connected to a particular religion. Its Twelve Steps refer to God or a “Higher Power”, but members understand that language in different ways.

Some members follow a religious faith. Others think of a Higher Power as the AA group, the recovery community, nature, shared wisdom or something beyond their individual willpower. Some remain agnostic or uncertain while participating in meetings.

You do not need to settle every spiritual question before attending. It is reasonable to listen, notice how different members interpret the programme and decide what is meaningful to you. You are also free to explore secular recovery groups or other support if AA’s approach does not fit your needs.

## Can I join if alcohol is not my only problem?

Many people seeking recovery have difficulties involving other drugs, mental health, trauma, gambling or relationships. Having another problem does not automatically prevent someone from joining AA if they also have a desire to stop drinking.

However, AA meetings focus on recovery from alcohol. Other fellowships, specialist services or healthcare professionals may be better placed to address additional concerns. Some people build a wider support network that includes AA, counselling, medical care, mental health support and another relevant peer group.

Taking prescribed medication does not by itself determine whether you can seek AA support. Decisions about medication belong between you and a qualified healthcare professional. Do not stop or change prescribed treatment because of another member’s personal opinion.

## Is AA free, and do I need a sponsor?

AA does not charge membership fees. Meetings may take voluntary contributions from members to cover costs such as room hire, refreshments or online services, but a newcomer should not need to pay to attend.

You also do not need a sponsor before joining. In AA, a **sponsor** is usually a member with experience of recovery who supports another person in working through the Twelve Steps. Sponsorship is not professional therapy, medical care or legal advice.

You can attend meetings, listen and take time to meet people before asking someone to sponsor you. If you want a clearer picture of meetings, sponsorship and early actions, see our [newcomer’s guide to starting recovery with Alcoholics Anonymous](/blog/how-to-start-your-recovery-with-alcoholics-anonymous).

## What if I am not sure I want to stop forever?

The idea of never drinking again can feel overwhelming. Many AA members approach sobriety one day at a time rather than trying to emotionally solve the rest of their lives at once.

A desire to stop drinking does not mean you must arrive with complete confidence. You may feel frightened, doubtful or conflicted. What matters is being honest about where alcohol has taken you and whether you want something to change.

An open meeting can be a lower-pressure starting point if you are still uncertain. You can listen to other people’s experiences without promising to join, speak or work the Steps.

## How to take the first step towards a meeting

If you think AA may be relevant, keep the first action simple:

1. Find a local or online meeting through an official AA meeting directory.
2. Check whether it is open or closed and note any accessibility information.
3. Arrive a few minutes early or contact the meeting beforehand if you are anxious.
4. Tell the person welcoming attendees that it is your first meeting.
5. Listen for similarities rather than expecting every story to match yours.
6. Consider trying several meetings, as their size, format and atmosphere can differ.

You do not need to share personal details. You may be invited to speak, but newcomers can generally say that they would prefer to listen. Protect other attendees’ privacy by not identifying them or repeating their personal stories outside the meeting.

## A note about alcohol withdrawal and medical safety

AA can offer fellowship and practical peer support, but it does not replace medical assessment or supervised withdrawal care.

If you drink heavily or regularly, have previously experienced withdrawal, or develop symptoms when alcohol wears off, seek professional guidance before suddenly stopping. Alcohol withdrawal can be dangerous for some dependent drinkers. Contact a GP, NHS service or local alcohol treatment provider for advice. If symptoms are severe or there is an immediate risk to life, seek emergency medical help.

## Conclusion

Anyone with a desire to stop drinking can consider membership of Alcoholics Anonymous. You do not need to prove that your situation is severe enough, accept a label immediately or arrive with perfect confidence.

An appropriate AA meeting can help you learn from people with lived experience, while professional and alternative recovery support remain available too. The most important step is not deciding whether you fit a stereotype; it is responding safely and honestly to your concern about alcohol.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I attend AA if I drank recently?

You do not have to achieve a period of sobriety before seeking AA support. If you have been drinking, do not drive to a meeting. Contact the meeting or an AA helpline if you are unsure about local arrangements, and seek medical help if you are unwell or at risk from withdrawal.

### Can family members join Alcoholics Anonymous?

AA membership is for people who want to stop drinking. Family members or friends may attend open AA meetings, while closed meetings are for those with a desire to stop. Separate family support fellowships and local services may also be helpful.

### Do I need a doctor’s referral to join AA?

No referral is normally required. You can find and attend a suitable meeting yourself. Medical support may still be important, particularly if you may be physically dependent on alcohol or have other health concerns.

### Can I try AA without committing to the Twelve Steps?

Yes. You can attend a meeting, listen and decide whether the fellowship may help you. There is no need to understand or begin all Twelve Steps at your first meeting. Members often explore the programme gradually with support.
