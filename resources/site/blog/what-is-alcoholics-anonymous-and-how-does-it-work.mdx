Alcoholics Anonymous, commonly called AA, is a worldwide peer-support fellowship for people who want to stop drinking. Members support one another through regular AA meetings, shared experience, the Twelve Steps, sponsorship and service. There is no membership fee, formal enrollment process or requirement to accept a particular medical diagnosis.

AA is not professional treatment, and it does not provide medical care. It is one possible source of continuing recovery support, either on its own or alongside therapy, medication, rehabilitation, mutual-help alternatives and other services. Many people value its accessibility, community and practical focus on staying sober one day at a time.

If you may be physically dependent on alcohol, seek medical guidance before stopping suddenly. Alcohol withdrawal can be dangerous and sometimes life-threatening. A qualified healthcare professional or local alcohol and drug service can help you consider a safe approach.

## What is Alcoholics Anonymous?

Alcoholics Anonymous began in the United States in 1935 and has since developed into an international fellowship. Local groups hold meetings where people share their experiences of drinking, sobriety and recovery.

AA describes itself as a fellowship rather than a clinic, counseling service or religious organization. Meetings are generally organized by members rather than healthcare professionals. Members do not diagnose one another, prescribe treatment or provide detoxification.

The basic membership requirement is a desire to stop drinking. A person does not need to prove that they have reached a particular level of harm, use the label “alcoholic,” or wait until they have lost everything. If you are unsure whether alcohol is becoming a problem, these [signs and practical next steps](/blog/how-do-i-know-if-i-have-a-drinking-problem) may help you reflect.

The word “anonymous” has several meanings within AA. It protects members’ privacy, encourages people not to identify other attendees publicly and emphasizes equality rather than status. You may choose whether to tell others that you attend, but you should respect the privacy of everyone you meet there.

## How does AA work?

AA does not rely on one single activity. Its approach combines several forms of mutual support.

### Regular AA meetings

Meetings give participants a place to hear how other people have stopped drinking and handled life without alcohol. Depending on the format, one or more members may share their experiences, or everyone may have an opportunity to speak.

Listening is acceptable. Newcomers are not usually required to tell their full story, answer personal questions or make an immediate commitment. Customs differ between groups and countries, but respectful participation and confidentiality are widely valued.

Meetings may be:

- **In person or online:** Online meetings can be helpful when travel, disability, work, caregiving or location makes attendance difficult.
- **Open or closed:** Open meetings generally welcome anyone interested in learning about AA, including relatives or professionals. Closed meetings are intended for people who have a desire to stop drinking. Local descriptions should clarify the format.
- **Discussion meetings:** Members discuss recovery experiences or a suggested topic.
- **Speaker meetings:** One or more members tell their recovery stories.
- **Step or literature meetings:** The group discusses the Twelve Steps or AA literature.

The atmosphere varies. One meeting may feel quiet and reflective, while another is informal and lively. A disappointing first meeting does not necessarily represent AA as a whole.

### The Twelve Steps

The Twelve Steps are AA’s suggested program of personal recovery. They involve acknowledging that drinking has become unmanageable, becoming open to help, examining behavior, addressing harm where appropriate, developing daily self-awareness and supporting others.

The Steps are generally worked gradually rather than completed in a single meeting. They are suggestions within AA, not an admission test. Newcomers can attend while they learn what the language means and decide whether the approach is useful.

Some Steps refer to God or a “Higher Power.” AA is spiritual in its traditional language, but it does not require membership in a religion. Members interpret a Higher Power in different ways: as God, the AA group, human connection, nature or a source of guidance beyond individual willpower. Secular and atheist-oriented meetings are also available in many places.

For a clearer overview, see [the Twelve Steps of Alcoholics Anonymous explained simply](/blog/the-twelve-steps-of-alcoholics-anonymous-explained).

### Sponsorship

A sponsor is an AA member who informally helps another member understand and apply the Twelve Steps. Sponsors usually draw on personal recovery experience rather than professional qualifications.

Sponsorship is normally voluntary and unpaid. A sponsor may listen, answer questions, discuss Step work and encourage meeting attendance. They should not control your relationships, finances, healthcare or major life decisions.

A sponsor is not a doctor, therapist or emergency service. Medical symptoms, trauma, severe mental distress and medication decisions should be discussed with an appropriately qualified professional. It is reasonable to change sponsors if the relationship feels unsafe, controlling or unhelpful.

### Service and helping others

AA members often take practical roles such as preparing a meeting room, welcoming newcomers, helping with online access or serving on a group committee. This is called “service.”

Service can strengthen connection and give structure to recovery. More experienced members may also support newcomers because helping others is part of AA’s Twelve Step approach. No newcomer should feel pressured to accept responsibilities before they are ready.

### One day at a time

AA commonly encourages members to focus on not drinking today rather than trying to solve the rest of their lives at once. This can make sobriety feel more manageable, especially during stressful periods.

The principle does not mean ignoring long-term planning. It means bringing attention back to the actions available now: attending a meeting, calling someone supportive, eating, resting, keeping a healthcare appointment or moving away from a high-risk situation.

## What happens at your first AA meeting?

Most meetings begin with a welcome and a brief explanation of the format. Participants may introduce themselves by first name. The group might then hear a reading, a speaker or contributions from members.

Some meetings include prayer, often at the beginning or end. Participation is generally optional. You can remain silent if particular spiritual language does not fit your beliefs.

You may hear members identify themselves as “alcoholics.” This is traditional AA language and usually reflects how those individuals understand their own experience. You do not have to adopt the label before attending or seeking help.

Meetings are typically funded through voluntary contributions from members. Newcomers are not normally expected to contribute, and lack of money should not prevent attendance. AA does not charge membership dues.

If you want to try a meeting, practical first steps include:

1. Use an official AA website or local intergroup directory to find a current meeting.
2. Check whether it is open or closed, in person or online, and whether it has a particular focus.
3. Arrive a few minutes early or log in before the start.
4. Tell the meeting contact or greeter that you are new if you feel comfortable.
5. Listen for experiences that are useful rather than expecting every viewpoint to match yours.
6. Consider trying several groups before deciding whether AA suits you.

## Does Alcoholics Anonymous work for everyone?

No recovery approach works for every person. Some people find that frequent meetings, supportive relationships and the Twelve Steps help them maintain long-term sobriety. Others prefer professionally led treatment, secular mutual-help groups, medication, individual therapy or a combination of approaches.

Evidence suggests that well-structured AA and Twelve Step Facilitation—which is professional treatment designed to encourage AA participation—can help some people achieve abstinence. Outcomes vary, and participation tends to be more useful when a person finds meetings they can engage with consistently.

AA may be especially appealing if you want free, widely available peer support from people with lived experience. It may feel less suitable if you strongly dislike its spiritual language, want professional clinical guidance within meetings or do not have access to a group where you feel safe and respected.

You do not have to choose between AA and healthcare. Many members also use therapy, medical treatment or other recovery services. Medication for alcohol use disorder is a healthcare matter and should be discussed with a qualified professional, not stopped or changed because of an opinion expressed at a meeting.

## How to decide whether AA may help you

You can explore AA without making a permanent commitment. Try several meetings, notice how you feel before and afterward, and ask whether the group offers respect, hope and practical support.

Useful questions include:

- Can I relate to some of the experiences shared?
- Do I feel welcomed without being pressured?
- Are privacy and personal boundaries respected?
- Does attending make it easier to avoid drinking today?
- Can I combine this support with any professional care I need?

If one group does not fit, another format may. Online, secular, women’s, men’s, LGBTQ+, young people’s and language-specific meetings are available in many regions, although local options differ.

## Conclusion

Alcoholics Anonymous is a free, international peer fellowship based on meetings, mutual support, the Twelve Steps, sponsorship and service. It offers community rather than medical treatment and can be used alongside other recovery options.

You do not need to reach “rock bottom” or understand every part of AA before attending. A first meeting can simply be an opportunity to listen, ask questions and see whether this form of support helps you take the next safe step.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I have to call myself an alcoholic to attend AA?

No. Members often use that term for themselves, but you do not have to accept a label before exploring AA. The membership requirement is a desire to stop drinking. Closed meetings are for people with that desire, while open meetings may also welcome visitors.

### Is Alcoholics Anonymous religious?

AA is not affiliated with a particular religion, but its traditional Steps use spiritual language, including references to God and a Higher Power. Interpretations vary widely. People from many faiths, as well as agnostic and atheist members, attend AA. Secular meeting formats are available in many areas.

### Can I attend AA if I am still drinking?

Yes. You do not need to achieve sobriety before asking for help or attending an appropriate meeting. However, individual groups may have practical expectations about disruptive behavior. If you are intoxicated, at immediate risk or experiencing possible withdrawal, seek suitable medical or emergency support as well.

### Is AA a replacement for detox, therapy or medical treatment?

No. AA provides peer support, not diagnosis, detoxification or clinical care. If you experience shaking, sweating, vomiting, confusion, seizures, hallucinations or other concerning symptoms after reducing alcohol, contact local emergency services or a qualified healthcare professional. Recovery can include AA alongside medical care, therapy and other support.
