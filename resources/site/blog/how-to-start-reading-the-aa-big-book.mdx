The simplest way to start reading the AA Big Book is to begin with **“The Doctor’s Opinion,” followed by Chapters 1 through 5**, reading only a few pages at a time. You do not have to understand everything immediately or finish the entire book before attending an AA meeting. Focus on identifying what feels relevant, noting your questions and discussing the material with an experienced AA member or sponsor if possible.

The book can feel old-fashioned, spiritual or surprisingly dense when you first open it. That is normal. Treat it as a practical recovery text to explore rather than an exam you must pass. Reading regularly, honestly and with support is usually more helpful than trying to absorb it all in one sitting.

If you may be physically dependent on alcohol, remember that reading recovery literature is not a substitute for medical care. Abruptly stopping alcohol can be dangerous for some people. Seek guidance from a qualified healthcare professional or local alcohol and drug support service if withdrawal could be a concern.

## What is the AA Big Book?

The AA Big Book is the informal name for *Alcoholics Anonymous*, the foundational book of the Alcoholics Anonymous fellowship. It explains AA’s view of the alcohol problem, describes the Twelve Step approach and includes personal recovery stories.

The nickname “Big Book” came from the physically large format of its first edition. Today, it is available in different formats, editions and languages.

In the standard English edition, the foundational material is commonly described as the “first 164 pages.” Page numbering and formatting may differ in translations, digital versions and accessible editions. Personal stories can also vary between editions and countries.

The Big Book was first published in 1939, so some expressions reflect the social attitudes and writing style of that period. Readers can still engage with its central recovery ideas without agreeing with every phrase, example or historical assumption.

## Where should a beginner start?

You can read from the beginning, but you do not need to spend a long time studying every preface or edition note before reaching the main text. A practical newcomer reading order is:

1. **“The Doctor’s Opinion”**
2. **Chapter 1: “Bill’s Story”**
3. **Chapter 2: “There Is a Solution”**
4. **Chapter 3: “More About Alcoholism”**
5. **Chapter 4: “We Agnostics”**
6. **Chapter 5: “How It Works”**
7. **Chapters 6 and 7: “Into Action” and “Working With Others”**

The first three chapters introduce the book’s description of compulsive drinking and the possibility of recovery. Chapter 4 addresses spiritual questions, while Chapters 5 and 6 move into the Twelve Steps and practical action.

You can find a more detailed overview in this guide to the [best AA Big Book chapters for newcomers](/blog/best-big-book-chapters-for-newcomers).

### Do not rush into every exercise alone

The Big Book contains directions connected with personal inventory, sharing difficult experiences, making amends and helping others. These are not simply reading assignments.

For example, Step Four involves writing an inventory, while Steps Eight and Nine concern harms and amends. These actions can involve sensitive relationships, trauma, finances, legal issues or personal safety. A sponsor or another trusted, experienced person can help you consider them carefully. Professional support may also be appropriate.

Reading ahead is fine. Acting impulsively on everything you read is not necessary.

## A simple seven-session reading plan

You can complete this plan over seven days, seven weeks or any schedule that feels manageable. Consistency matters more than speed.

### Session 1: Understand the book’s purpose

Read the relevant preface for your edition and “The Doctor’s Opinion.” Ask:

- How does the book describe the physical and mental sides of the alcohol problem?
- Which parts resemble my experience?
- Which parts do not?

The medical language is historical and should not be used to diagnose yourself. Questions about alcohol use disorder, dependence or withdrawal should be discussed with a qualified healthcare professional.

### Session 2: Read “Bill’s Story”

This chapter presents one co-founder’s experience. You do not need to have the same history, losses or spiritual experience.

Look for patterns rather than exact similarities: attempts to control drinking, promises to stop, consequences, isolation and changing attitudes toward help.

### Session 3: Read “There Is a Solution”

Notice how the chapter moves from describing a problem to describing fellowship, shared experience and a program of action.

Ask yourself what kinds of support you currently have. Reading can be useful, but recovery is often strengthened by human connection, whether through AA, professional treatment, another mutual-help group or a combination of approaches.

### Session 4: Read “More About Alcoholism”

This chapter examines repeated returns to drinking despite sincere intentions. You might note any examples that help you understand your own decision-making.

Try not to use the chapter to prove that your situation is “bad enough.” You do not have to reach a catastrophic rock bottom before asking for help.

### Session 5: Read “We Agnostics”

This chapter addresses belief and spirituality. Some newcomers appreciate it immediately; others struggle with its assumptions or language.

You are allowed to have questions. In AA, “Higher Power” generally refers to a source of help beyond isolated self-reliance, but individual members understand that idea in different ways. You do not have to force certainty or adopt another person’s beliefs to continue learning.

### Session 6: Read “How It Works”

This chapter introduces the Twelve Steps and begins explaining how they are applied. Read slowly. Mark unfamiliar expressions such as “inventory,” “amends” or “spiritual awakening” for later discussion.

For a plain-language overview, see [the Twelve Steps of Alcoholics Anonymous explained simply](/blog/the-twelve-steps-of-alcoholics-anonymous-explained).

### Session 7: Review rather than race ahead

Return to the passages you marked. Write down:

- One idea that felt accurate
- One idea you resisted
- One question you want to ask
- One safe action you could take today

That action might be attending an AA meeting, contacting a supportive person, finding a sponsor or arranging professional guidance.

## How to read actively without overcomplicating it

You do not need an elaborate study system. A book, notebook and 10 to 20 quiet minutes can be enough.

### Use four simple questions

After each section, ask:

1. What does this say about the alcohol problem?
2. What does it say about recovery?
3. Is it suggesting an action?
4. What do I want to discuss with someone else?

These questions keep your attention on meaning and application rather than trying to memorize passages.

### Mark reactions, not just important sentences

A passage that irritates, confuses or frightens you may deserve as much attention as one that inspires you. You might use brief symbols:

- A question mark for something unclear
- An exclamation point for something that feels important
- An “R” for something you relate to
- An “A” beside a possible action

If you are using a borrowed meeting copy, keep notes separately rather than writing in it.

### Translate ideas into your own words

After a few pages, pause and summarize what you think the author is saying. Plain language makes it easier to discover whether you understand the point or merely recognize the words.

You can also ask, “What might this look like in my life today?” This helps connect a text written decades ago with present-day recovery.

## Reading with an AA sponsor or group

A sponsor is an AA member who informally shares their experience of working the Twelve Steps. Sponsorship is not therapy, medical treatment or professional case management. It is also not a relationship in which someone should control your healthcare, money, employment or personal life.

A sponsor can explain unfamiliar AA language, describe how they applied a chapter and help you distinguish reading from taking Step actions. If you do not yet have a sponsor, you can still begin reading and ask people at meetings how they approached the book.

When reading with someone, agree on a manageable section in advance. Bring your questions, listen to their experience and remain honest about what you do not understand or believe. This [practical guide to reading the Big Book with a sponsor](/blog/how-to-read-the-big-book-with-a-sponsor) explains how to structure those conversations.

Some AA meetings are dedicated to Big Book study. Formats vary: a group may read a short passage and invite members to share their experience, or it may study one chapter over several meetings. You may listen without speaking if that is permitted by the meeting’s format.

## Common mistakes to avoid

### Trying to understand everything immediately

The book often becomes clearer through meetings, discussion and lived experience. Confusion at the beginning does not mean you are failing.

### Treating every sentence as personalised medical advice

The Big Book is recovery literature, not a current medical textbook. Use qualified healthcare professionals for questions about diagnosis, medication, mental health and withdrawal.

### Comparing your losses with other people’s stories

You do not need to match another person’s drinking history. Pay attention to patterns, consequences and loss of control rather than deciding whether your experience was dramatic enough.

### Getting stuck on one objection

You can note a disagreement and keep reading. It is possible to gain something from the broader program while questioning particular wording or viewpoints.

### Reading without building support

Books can provide understanding, but they cannot call you during a craving or notice when you are becoming isolated. Consider combining reading with meetings, supportive relationships, healthcare or other evidence-informed recovery resources.

## The Big Book is a beginning, not a test

Start small: read “The Doctor’s Opinion” and the first few chapters, underline what speaks to you and write down what does not make sense. Then discuss your reactions with someone trustworthy.

You do not need perfect belief, perfect concentration or a dramatic recovery story. A willingness to read honestly and take one safe next step is enough for today. AA is one recovery pathway, and it can also be used alongside appropriate medical, psychological and community support.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I have to read the AA Big Book from cover to cover?

No. Many newcomers begin with the foundational chapters and return to the personal stories later. Reading in order provides context, but discussion and practical application are more important than finishing quickly.

### How much of the Big Book should I read each day?

A few pages or 10 to 20 minutes is a reasonable starting point. Stop when your attention fades, note one useful idea and continue later. There is no prize for reading it rapidly.

### Can I read the Big Book if I am not religious?

Yes. You may encounter explicitly spiritual and religious language, and you are free to question it. Many readers explore concepts such as a Higher Power gradually and interpret spirituality in ways that are meaningful to them.

### Which edition of the AA Big Book should I use?

Use a current edition or authorized translation that you can read comfortably. Print, digital, large-print and audio formats may be available through official AA publishing channels, local AA service offices or meetings. The personal stories and page layouts may differ, but the foundational recovery text remains the main focus.
