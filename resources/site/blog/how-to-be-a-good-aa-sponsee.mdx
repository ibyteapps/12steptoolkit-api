Being a good AA sponsee is not about being perfect, agreeing with everything your sponsor says or giving another person control over your life. It means approaching sponsorship with honesty, willingness, regular communication and a readiness to take practical action.

In Alcoholics Anonymous (AA), a sponsor is usually a member who has experience with sobriety and the Twelve Steps and is willing to share that experience one to one. The person receiving that support is often called a **sponsee**. Sponsorship is an informal relationship rather than professional treatment, and its exact form varies between people, groups and cultures.

A productive sponsee takes responsibility for their own recovery while remaining open to guidance. The following principles can help you build a respectful, useful sponsorship relationship.

## 1. Choose a sponsor thoughtfully

A sponsor does not need to be impressive, charismatic or exactly like you. Look for someone whose recovery appears stable and whose conduct reflects qualities you would like to develop, such as honesty, patience, reliability and humility.

It can help to ask how the person approaches the Twelve Steps, how often they normally speak with sponsees and what they expect from the relationship. Some sponsors prefer scheduled calls; others use a more flexible arrangement. Neither approach is automatically better, but you need to know what you are agreeing to.

A sponsor should not promise to solve every problem or guarantee that you will stay sober. Their role is generally to share their experience of AA and help you work through the program. If sponsorship is still unfamiliar, this guide to [how Alcoholics Anonymous works](/blog/what-is-alcoholics-anonymous-and-how-does-it-work) explains meetings, Steps and sponsorship for newcomers.

## 2. Discuss expectations early

Many sponsorship problems begin with unspoken assumptions. One person may expect daily contact while the other thinks a weekly conversation is enough.

Early in the relationship, consider asking:

- How often should we contact each other?
- Is it better to call at a set time?
- How will we work through the Twelve Steps?
- What should I do if I feel at risk of drinking and cannot reach you?
- Are there times when you are unavailable?
- How do we approach privacy and sensitive information?

These questions are not demanding. They create clarity and reduce avoidable misunderstandings. Expectations may also change as your recovery develops, so revisit them when necessary.

## 3. Make contact consistently

Regular contact helps sponsorship become a genuine recovery practice rather than an emergency service used only during a crisis. If you agree to call, make a reasonable effort to call. If you miss an arranged conversation, acknowledge it and reschedule rather than disappearing through embarrassment.

You do not need to have something dramatic to report. A brief, honest conversation about your mood, meetings, cravings, resentments or Step work can reveal problems before they grow.

Consistency also teaches an important recovery skill: asking for support before isolation takes over.

## 4. Be as honest as you can

A sponsor can only respond to what you share. Hiding drinking, cravings, relationship problems or resentment because you want to appear to be a “good” sponsee limits the value of sponsorship.

Honesty does not always arrive at once. You may need time to build trust, especially if you have experienced criticism, betrayal or trauma. You can begin by saying, “There is something I am finding difficult to talk about.” That itself is an honest starting point.

If you drink, tell your sponsor rather than waiting until you have created a more acceptable version of the story. A return to alcohol deserves a practical and compassionate response, not humiliation.

## 5. Be willing to take action

Sponsorship is more than discussing recovery ideas. A sponsor may suggest attending meetings, reading AA material, writing about a Step, contacting another member or making a simple daily plan.

Being willing does not mean following every suggestion without thought. It means considering guidance seriously, asking questions and trying reasonable actions rather than repeatedly requesting advice and then dismissing it.

The Steps can initially seem abstract or intimidating. A newcomer-friendly explanation of [the Twelve Steps of Alcoholics Anonymous](/blog/the-twelve-steps-of-alcoholics-anonymous-explained) can help you understand their overall purpose before exploring them with a sponsor.

## 6. Ask questions instead of pretending to understand

AA has its own language, including terms such as “inventory,” “amends,” “Higher Power” and “one day at a time.” A good sponsorship relationship leaves room for questions.

If an instruction is unclear, ask your sponsor to explain what it means and how they applied it in their own recovery. If spiritual language feels uncomfortable, say so. AA includes people with many different religious, spiritual and non-religious viewpoints, although local meeting cultures vary.

You are not failing because you need clarification. Thoughtful questions often indicate that you are engaging seriously with the process.

## 7. Listen with an open mind—but retain your judgment

Sponsors share personal experience; they are not official AA authorities. What worked for your sponsor may be helpful, but it is not automatically the only valid approach.

Listen for the principle behind a suggestion. If your sponsor recommends calling someone when you feel isolated, for example, the principle may be connection rather than obeying a rigid rule.

You remain responsible for decisions involving your health, employment, finances, housing, legal affairs and relationships. A sponsor should not replace qualified medical, psychological, legal or financial advice.

## 8. Respect time and boundaries

Sponsors are volunteers with their own recovery, responsibilities and personal lives. Unless you have agreed otherwise, avoid expecting immediate availability at every hour. Ask what contact arrangements work for both of you.

Healthy boundaries apply in both directions. A sponsor should not pressure you for money, control your relationships, demand access to private accounts, pursue sexual or romantic contact through the sponsorship role, or insist that you ignore professional advice.

You can value your sponsor’s help without surrendering your autonomy. Respect is mutual, not one-sided.

## 9. Build a wider recovery network

One person should not have to carry your entire support system. Attend AA meetings, learn the names of other members and collect several phone numbers where this is customary. You may also benefit from healthcare, counseling, peer support outside AA, community programs or another evidence-based recovery pathway.

This wider network matters when your sponsor is unavailable, traveling, unwell or dealing with personal responsibilities. It also gives you access to a range of experiences rather than relying on a single viewpoint.

AA can be an important part of recovery, but it is not the only route to sobriety and does not provide clinical treatment for alcohol use disorder.

## 10. Speak directly about disagreement

You will not always agree with your sponsor. Disagreement does not necessarily mean the relationship has failed.

Try to be direct and respectful: “I am unsure about that suggestion,” or “Can you help me understand why you think this would help?” Your sponsor may clarify their reasoning, offer an alternative or acknowledge that the decision belongs to you.

Avoid silently building resentment while pretending everything is fine. Honest disagreement can strengthen trust when both people handle it with maturity.

If a sponsor reacts to reasonable questions with threats, ridicule or repeated pressure, consider speaking with trusted AA members and reassessing the relationship.

## 11. Do the work between conversations

A sponsor can offer direction, but they cannot attend meetings, complete written reflection or practise sober behavior on your behalf. Try to follow through on what you have agreed to do and report honestly when you have not done it.

Simple preparation can make conversations more useful. Before calling, ask yourself:

- What has supported my sobriety since we last spoke?
- Where have I struggled?
- Is there anything I am avoiding?
- What action have I taken?
- What question do I need to ask?

This keeps sponsorship focused without making it rigid or formal.

## 12. Practise appreciation and service

Your sponsor does not need constant praise, but a sincere thank-you matters. Respecting their time, following through and being honest are meaningful ways to show appreciation.

As your recovery grows, service can also become part of your own practice. That might mean welcoming newcomers, helping prepare a meeting space or taking another small meeting responsibility. Sponsoring someone else generally comes later, when you have relevant experience and sufficient stability. This practical guide explains [how sponsors can support someone through the Twelve Steps](/blog/how-to-sponsor-someone-through-the-twelve-steps).

## What being a good sponsee does not mean

Being a good sponsee does **not** require you to:

- Obey every instruction without question
- Reveal personal information before you feel safe
- Accept humiliation or aggressive confrontation
- Treat your sponsor as a doctor, therapist or spiritual authority
- Stay in a relationship that feels controlling or unsafe
- Achieve flawless recovery

Willingness and accountability are valuable, but they should exist alongside dignity, consent and sensible boundaries.

## When the sponsorship relationship is not working

Sometimes a sponsor and sponsee are simply not a good fit. Communication styles, availability or approaches to the Steps may differ. If possible, discuss the difficulty directly before ending the arrangement.

If you decide to change sponsors, be courteous and clear. You do not need to create a case against the other person. You might say, “Thank you for the help you have given me. I think I need a different sponsorship approach now.” You can then speak with other experienced members before choosing someone new.

Leave promptly if there is manipulation, harassment, discrimination, financial exploitation or pressure to enter a sexual or romantic relationship. Seek support from trusted people and meeting organizers where appropriate.

## Keep medical safety separate from sponsorship

A sponsor may share personal recovery experience, but they are not a substitute for a healthcare professional. Abruptly stopping alcohol can be medically dangerous for some people who have become physically dependent.

If you are drinking heavily, have experienced withdrawal symptoms or are unsure whether stopping is safe, seek guidance from a qualified healthcare professional or local alcohol and drug service. Severe symptoms such as seizures, hallucinations, extreme confusion or loss of consciousness require urgent help from local emergency services.

## Conclusion

A good AA sponsee is not a perfect follower. They are an active participant in their own recovery: honest where possible, willing to learn, consistent in communication and respectful of healthy boundaries.

Choose a sponsor carefully, ask clear questions and take practical action. At the same time, build a wider support network and remember that responsibility for your health and decisions remains with you. Strong sponsorship is a partnership grounded in shared experience, trust and steady growth.

## Frequently asked questions

### How often should an AA sponsee call their sponsor?

There is no universal schedule. Some sponsors suggest daily calls in early sobriety, while others arrange contact several times a week or as needed. Agree on a realistic pattern together, including what to do when your sponsor is unavailable.

### Do I have to do everything my AA sponsor says?

No. A sponsor offers experience and guidance, not commands. Consider suggestions seriously and ask questions, but retain responsibility for your decisions. Medical, legal, financial and mental health concerns should be discussed with appropriately qualified professionals.

### Can I change my AA sponsor?

Yes. Sponsorship is voluntary, and you may change sponsors if the relationship is unhelpful, incompatible or unsafe. When possible, communicate your decision respectfully. You do not need permission to leave a sponsorship relationship.

### What should I talk about with my sponsor?

You can discuss Step work, meetings, cravings, fears, resentments, relationships, sober routines and situations that may threaten your recovery. Aim for honesty rather than trying to sound successful. A sponsor can be helpful, but serious health or safety concerns require professional support.
