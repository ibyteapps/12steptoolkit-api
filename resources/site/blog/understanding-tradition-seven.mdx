Tradition Seven of Alcoholics Anonymous explains how AA groups support themselves financially while protecting their independence. Its short form says: **“Every A.A. group ought to be fully self-supporting, declining outside contributions.”**

In practical terms, this means AA groups rely on voluntary contributions from their own members rather than funding from governments, businesses, charities or wealthy outside supporters. The money pays ordinary expenses such as meeting-room rent, online meeting services, literature and contributions to AA service bodies.

Nobody has to donate to attend an AA meeting or belong to Alcoholics Anonymous. Tradition Seven is about shared responsibility, not an admission fee. By meeting their own expenses, groups reduce the risk that outside funding could influence their purpose or decisions.

## What is Tradition Seven of Alcoholics Anonymous?

Alcoholics Anonymous has Twelve Traditions that guide the life and organization of its groups. The Twelve Steps focus mainly on personal recovery, while the Traditions help groups maintain unity, independence and a clear primary purpose.

If you are new to the fellowship, this distinction can be helpful:

- **The Twelve Steps** offer a suggested program of personal recovery.
- **The Twelve Traditions** guide how AA groups relate to members, money, service and the wider world.
- **A group conscience** is the process through which group members discuss an issue and reach an informed collective decision.

Tradition Seven asks each group to take responsibility for its own costs. It also asks AA groups and service bodies to avoid becoming financially dependent on outside organizations.

For a broader introduction to meetings, membership and the recovery program, see [what Alcoholics Anonymous is and how AA works](/blog/what-is-alcoholics-anonymous-and-how-does-it-work).

## Why does AA decline outside contributions?

Money can bring expectations, obligations and influence, even when it is offered with good intentions. A donor may want recognition, a say in group decisions or control over how funds are used. A group that depends on a government agency, treatment provider, religious organization or private benefactor could find it harder to act independently.

Declining outside contributions helps an AA group remain focused on its primary purpose: carrying its recovery message to people who want help with a drinking problem.

This does not mean AA is hostile toward healthcare providers, community organizations, treatment services or other recovery pathways. AA groups may cooperate with outside organizations without affiliating with or being controlled by them. People may also combine AA participation with therapy, medication, medical care, peer-support programs or other forms of recovery support.

Self-support creates a practical boundary: AA can cooperate with others while paying its own way and making its own decisions.

## What does “fully self-supporting” mean in practice?

At many in-person AA meetings, a basket is passed around for voluntary contributions. Some online or hybrid groups provide a digital payment option. Members may contribute whatever they choose, including nothing.

A group’s expenses might include:

- Rent for a community room or other meeting space
- Video-conferencing or website costs
- Tea, coffee and basic meeting supplies
- AA literature for newcomers or group use
- Accessibility expenses, where available and agreed by the group
- Contributions to local, regional, national or international AA services
- A reasonable financial reserve for future expenses

The exact arrangements differ between groups and countries. A small online meeting may have very few costs, while a large in-person group may pay substantial rent. Each autonomous group decides through its group conscience how to manage its funds within the Traditions.

Being self-supporting does not require every meeting to accumulate a large bank balance. Many groups aim to pay current expenses, maintain a prudent reserve and send surplus funds to AA service bodies that support work beyond the individual meeting.

## Is contributing money required at an AA meeting?

No. AA does not charge membership dues or meeting fees. A person can attend without donating, and nobody should be excluded, embarrassed or treated differently because they cannot contribute.

This matters especially for newcomers. Someone arriving at an AA meeting may be unemployed, facing debt, living in temporary accommodation or simply unsure whether AA is right for them. The contribution basket should not become a source of pressure.

If you are new, it is reasonable to pass the basket without contributing. You can participate in other ways, such as helping put away chairs, welcoming another newcomer or staying to listen. Financial participation may become possible later, but belonging is not purchased.

AA’s membership principle is separate from financial contribution. Our guide to [who can join Alcoholics Anonymous](/blog/who-can-join-alcoholics-anonymous) explains this in more detail.

## The spiritual principles behind Tradition Seven

Although Tradition Seven deals with money, its meaning is broader than bookkeeping. It reflects several recovery principles.

### Responsibility

A group accepts responsibility for maintaining the meeting from which its members benefit. Even small contributions can represent participation in a shared effort.

### Independence

Paying its own expenses allows a group to make decisions without pressure from an external funder. Independence helps protect AA’s identity and purpose.

### Equality

A group is not meant to belong to its largest donor. Members have an equal voice in the group conscience whether they contribute a large amount, a small amount or nothing.

### Gratitude

Some members view contributing as a practical expression of gratitude for support they have received. This remains a personal choice rather than a requirement.

### Simplicity

Tradition Seven encourages groups to focus on what they genuinely need. Money is treated as a useful tool for service, not as a measure of success or status.

## How AA groups can handle money responsibly

Self-support works best when it is paired with openness and accountability. Groups commonly appoint a trusted servant—often called a treasurer—to look after funds and report regularly to members.

Good practices may include:

1. **Agreeing on essential expenses.** The group conscience can decide what the meeting needs and what it can reasonably afford.
2. **Providing clear reports.** Members should be able to understand income, spending, reserves and service contributions.
3. **Using more than one safeguard.** Where practical, groups can avoid placing complete financial control with one person.
4. **Keeping a prudent reserve.** A modest reserve can cover expected rent or other costs if contributions temporarily fall.
5. **Reviewing digital payment arrangements.** Online groups should consider privacy, account access, fees and continuity when a trusted servant rotates out.
6. **Following local AA guidance.** Contribution procedures and financial limits may vary among AA service structures around the world.
7. **Rotating service positions.** Rotation can reduce dependency on one individual and give other members an opportunity to serve.

AA service roles carry responsibility, but they do not create a governing class. This connects closely with [Tradition Nine and AA service without hierarchy](/blog/understanding-tradition-nine).

## Tradition Seven in online and hybrid AA meetings

The principle remains the same when a meeting takes place online. Digital groups may need to pay for video-conferencing accounts, web hosting, accessibility tools or other technology.

Because no physical basket is passed, the group might share an electronic contribution method during its regular announcements. Privacy deserves attention. Members should not be expected to reveal personal financial information to the whole group, and access to digital accounts should be managed carefully.

An online group can use its group conscience to answer practical questions:

- Which costs are genuinely necessary?
- Who can access the payment account?
- How will the treasurer report income and expenses?
- What happens when service roles rotate?
- Where will surplus funds go?

The technology changes, but the central idea does not: members voluntarily support their own group so that it can remain available and independent.

## Common misunderstandings about the Seventh Tradition

### “The person who gives the most should have the most influence”

Financial contributions do not buy authority. AA decisions are made through group conscience, not according to the size of a member’s donation.

### “A newcomer should contribute immediately”

A newcomer is welcome whether or not they donate. Groups can explain the basket briefly without applying pressure or tracking individual contributions.

### “Self-support means a group cannot cooperate with anyone”

AA groups can communicate and cooperate with healthcare facilities, correctional institutions, community centers and other organizations. The important distinction is between cooperation and financial dependence or affiliation.

### “An AA group should never spend money”

Tradition Seven does not require poverty. Rent, communication tools, literature and service activities cost money. The principle is to use funds responsibly in support of AA’s purpose.

### “Free meeting space never raises a question”

Arrangements involving donated space or services may deserve discussion. Groups can consult their local AA service resources and decide through an informed group conscience whether an arrangement is consistent with self-support and independence.

## Applying Tradition Seven beyond the contribution basket

The Traditions are designed to guide AA groups, not to impose rules on every part of a member’s private life. Even so, some people find personal value in the principles behind Tradition Seven.

In recovery, self-support may inspire someone to take manageable responsibility, contribute time as well as money, or become more honest about financial matters. It does not mean refusing legitimate help, rushing into employment, repaying every debt immediately or becoming financially independent overnight.

Personal finances can be complicated. Recovery may begin during unemployment, illness, legal difficulty or family disruption. Responsible action often develops gradually and may include seeking qualified financial, legal, medical or social support when appropriate.

Service can also be nonfinancial. Making coffee, setting up an online room, greeting people and taking a regular service position all help a group function. Money pays expenses, but members’ time and care create the meeting.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the main purpose of AA Tradition Seven?

Its main purpose is to help AA groups remain responsible and independent. By covering their own expenses and declining outside contributions, groups reduce the risk of external financial influence.

### How much should I put in the AA basket?

There is no required amount. Contribute only if you want to and can comfortably do so. You are equally welcome if you contribute nothing.

### Can a wealthy AA member pay all of a group’s expenses?

Groups generally try to avoid dependence on a single donor, even if that person is a member. Local AA service structures may also provide guidance on contribution limits. The group can discuss the situation through an informed group conscience.

### Where does Seventh Tradition money go?

It usually covers the group’s direct expenses first. After maintaining an agreed reserve, the group may contribute surplus funds to AA service bodies that help provide meeting information, literature services, public information and support for carrying the AA message more widely.

## Conclusion

Tradition Seven keeps AA’s financial arrangements simple: members voluntarily support their groups, no one pays for belonging, and outside funding is declined. The result is more than a balanced account. Self-support helps protect equality, responsibility, unity and freedom from outside control.

For newcomers, the most important point is straightforward: you do not need money to attend an AA meeting. You are welcome to listen, participate and decide whether Alcoholics Anonymous may be useful in your recovery.
