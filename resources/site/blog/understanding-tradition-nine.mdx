Tradition Nine of Alcoholics Anonymous explains how AA can coordinate meetings and services without becoming a controlling hierarchy. In simple terms, AA needs organization for practical tasks, but it does not need authorities who govern members or direct their personal recovery.

The Tradition states: **“AA, as such, ought never be organized; but we may create service boards or committees directly responsible to those they serve.”**

This does not mean that AA should be disorganized. Meetings still need rooms, schedules, financial records, literature and communication. Tradition Nine says these responsibilities should be handled through service rather than power. Committees exist to help AA groups carry the recovery message, and they remain accountable to the people and groups they support.

## What does AA Tradition Nine mean?

Tradition Nine separates **organization for service** from **organization for control**.

Alcoholics Anonymous does not have a central authority that decides whether individual members are recovering correctly. There is no AA official who can prescribe treatment, enforce the Twelve Steps or supervise a member’s private life. Participation in the Steps, sponsorship and service is voluntary.

At the same time, practical coordination is necessary. An AA group may need people to:

- Open and prepare the meeting room
- Manage online meeting access
- Keep basic financial records
- Pay rent or digital platform costs
- Arrange literature supplies
- Welcome newcomers
- Communicate with local service bodies
- Coordinate public information or contact with institutions

Tradition Nine allows groups to create roles, committees and service boards for these purposes. The important point is that these bodies are **directly responsible to those they serve**. Their purpose is not to rule AA but to make its work possible.

If you are unfamiliar with meetings, sponsorship or the Steps, [what Alcoholics Anonymous is and how AA works](/blog/what-is-alcoholics-anonymous-and-how-does-it-work) offers a newcomer-friendly overview.

## Why does AA avoid a traditional hierarchy?

Many organizations rely on managers, executives and formal chains of command. AA developed a different approach because recovery fellowship depends heavily on voluntary participation, equality and individual responsibility.

No member has authority over another member’s sobriety. A person with decades of recovery may have valuable experience, but that experience does not give them the right to control someone else. A sponsor can make suggestions and share what worked for them, but sponsorship is not management, therapy or medical supervision.

This approach also helps protect the equality of AA membership. Service experience, professional status, income, education or popularity should not make one person more important than another within the fellowship.

Tradition Nine therefore discourages the creation of a powerful professional leadership class within AA. Necessary authority is limited to clearly defined service responsibilities and remains answerable to the fellowship.

## “Never organized” does not mean no organization

The first part of Tradition Nine is sometimes misunderstood. Taken out of context, “ought never be organized” may sound as if AA meetings should avoid planning, procedures or accountability.

In practice, the second half of the Tradition clarifies the first. AA can create service boards and committees. The distinction is between organizing **AA membership itself** and organizing **the work needed to support AA**.

For example, a committee may decide how to distribute meeting information within an agreed budget. It should not decide which members are worthy of attending AA. A meeting chair may keep a discussion on time and apply the group’s agreed format. That role does not make the chair the personal authority over everyone in the room.

A useful summary is:

- **People are not governed.**
- **Service tasks are coordinated.**
- **Authority is limited by the role.**
- **Those providing service remain accountable.**

This balance allows AA to function without turning the fellowship into an institution that controls its members.

## How service roles work in an AA group

AA groups often use rotating service roles. Titles and procedures vary between countries, areas and individual groups, but common examples include secretary, chairperson, treasurer, literature coordinator, greeter or representative to a local service body.

These are service positions, not ranks. A treasurer may be trusted to maintain accurate records and report on group funds, but does not own or control the group. A chairperson may guide the meeting format but does not become the group’s permanent leader.

Many groups make important decisions through a **group conscience**. This is a meeting in which members discuss matters affecting the group and seek an informed, collective decision. It may cover issues such as meeting times, service appointments, accessibility, expenses or changes to the meeting format.

Healthy service usually includes:

- A clear description of each responsibility
- Regular reports to the group
- Transparent handling of contributions and expenses
- Opportunities for members to ask questions
- Rotation of service positions where practical
- Respect for the group conscience
- A way to address mistakes without humiliation

Rotation can help prevent one person from becoming indispensable or overly influential. It also gives more members an opportunity to participate and learn.

## What does “directly responsible to those they serve” mean?

This phrase is central to Tradition Nine. A committee receives responsibility from the groups or members it supports, rather than gaining independent power over them.

Accountability may include reporting activities, explaining decisions, following an agreed budget and listening when the fellowship raises concerns. The precise arrangements differ across AA service structures worldwide, but the principle remains consistent: a service body exists for the fellowship, not the other way around.

Direct responsibility also means that service should be responsive. If a committee’s procedures make it harder for groups to carry AA’s message, the people involved can review those procedures rather than defending them simply because “that is how it has always been done.”

Accountability does not require blame or hostility. Members can ask clear questions, request accurate reports and suggest changes while treating one another with dignity.

## Tradition Nine in an everyday AA meeting

Imagine that an AA group needs a new meeting location. Members discuss accessibility, cost, transport, privacy and availability during a group conscience. They ask two volunteers to contact possible venues and report back.

Those volunteers have enough authority to gather information. They do not automatically have authority to sign an expensive long-term agreement unless the group has specifically entrusted them with that decision.

In another example, an online meeting may appoint a small technical team. The team can manage access, respond to disruption and protect the meeting’s agreed boundaries. Its members should apply those responsibilities consistently rather than using technical control to silence people they personally dislike.

Tradition Nine asks a practical question in both situations: **Is this authority being used to complete an agreed service task, or to control people?**

## Common problems Tradition Nine can help address

### One person takes over every decision

Long experience can be valuable, but no individual should become the permanent owner of a group. Rotation, clear role limits and informed group consciences can distribute responsibility more fairly.

### A service role becomes a status symbol

AA service is intended to support recovery and help carry the message. When a title is used to demand special treatment, members can return to the principle that the role exists to serve.

### Nobody knows who is responsible

Avoiding hierarchy does not mean avoiding clarity. Groups can record who has accepted each task, what the task includes and when the role will be reviewed.

### A committee stops communicating

Regular, understandable reports help service bodies remain directly responsible. Transparency is particularly important where money, personal contact information or public communication is involved.

### Members disagree strongly

Tradition Nine does not guarantee conflict-free meetings. It offers a framework for handling disagreement: focus on the service task, consult the group conscience and avoid turning practical authority into personal power.

Related guidance in [AA Tradition Ten](/blog/understanding-tradition-ten) explains why AA groups avoid taking positions on outside controversies, while [Tradition Twelve’s principles-before-personalities approach](/blog/understanding-tradition-twelve) can help members navigate personal tensions.

## How members can apply Tradition Nine in service

You do not need extensive AA experience to begin learning from this Tradition. Practical ways to apply it include:

1. **Ask what the role is for.** Understand the task, its limits and who receives your report.
2. **Listen before deciding.** Gather relevant views, especially from people affected by the decision.
3. **Share information clearly.** Give brief, accurate reports rather than keeping knowledge to yourself.
4. **Separate principles from preferences.** Your preferred solution is not automatically the group’s decision.
5. **Invite participation.** Help newer members learn service tasks instead of making yourself essential.
6. **Accept rotation gracefully.** Leaving a role can be an important practice in humility and trust.
7. **Raise concerns respectfully.** Accountability can be firm without becoming personal or punitive.
8. **Remember the purpose.** Administration should support AA’s primary recovery purpose, not become an end in itself.

These ideas can also be useful outside meetings. In families, workplaces and community groups, a person can take responsibility without trying to control everyone involved.

## Tradition Nine is not Step Nine

Newcomers sometimes confuse the Twelve Traditions with the Twelve Steps. They serve different purposes.

The **Twelve Steps** provide a suggested program of personal recovery. **Step Nine** concerns making direct amends where appropriate, while avoiding further harm.

The **Twelve Traditions** guide the unity and functioning of AA groups and the fellowship as a whole. **Tradition Nine** concerns service organization, limited authority and accountability.

Neither should be used to pressure a member into unsafe action. AA is also not a substitute for medical care, mental health treatment or other forms of recovery support. People may combine AA with professional treatment, medication where prescribed, secular peer support or other approaches.

## Conclusion

Tradition Nine allows Alcoholics Anonymous to organize necessary services without creating authorities who govern individual members. Committees, boards and group roles coordinate practical work, but remain responsible to the people and groups they serve.

At its best, the Tradition creates a culture of clear responsibility without personal control. Members are invited to serve, report honestly, share authority and remember that every role exists to support recovery rather than status.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does Tradition Nine mean AA has no rules?

No. Individual groups may agree on meeting formats, safety boundaries and service procedures. Tradition Nine means that these arrangements support the group’s purpose rather than creating a hierarchy that controls members’ recovery or private lives.

### Why does AA have committees if it should not be organized?

Committees handle necessary service tasks such as communication, finances, literature distribution and meeting support. Tradition Nine permits this practical organization while requiring service bodies to remain accountable to those they serve.

### Can an AA chairperson tell members what to do?

A chairperson can guide the meeting according to its agreed format and address immediate disruptions within the boundaries given by the group. The role does not give that person general authority over members, their sponsors, treatment decisions or personal recovery.

### What should I do if an AA service role is being misused?

Raise the concern calmly through the group’s established process, such as speaking with a trusted servant or attending a group conscience. Focus on specific conduct, the limits of the role and the needs of the group. If behavior feels threatening, discriminatory or unsafe, protect your wellbeing and consider another meeting or appropriate local support.
