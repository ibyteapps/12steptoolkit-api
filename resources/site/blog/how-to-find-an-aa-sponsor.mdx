Finding an AA sponsor usually begins by attending several Alcoholics Anonymous meetings, listening to members who have experience with the Twelve Steps, and asking someone whose recovery you respect if they are available to sponsor you. You do not need to find the “perfect” person, and you do not have to wait until you know everyone well. A willing, dependable and respectful sponsor can help you begin working the Steps while you continue building a wider recovery network.

A sponsor is an AA member who shares their experience of staying sober and working the Twelve Steps. Sponsorship is an informal, person-to-person relationship rather than a professional service or an official AA appointment. A sponsor is not a therapist, physician, legal adviser or emergency contact, although they may encourage you to seek appropriate professional support.

## Where to look for an AA sponsor

The most common place to find a sponsor is at an AA meeting. Local in-person meetings can help you observe how people behave over time, while online meetings may connect you with potential sponsors beyond your immediate area.

You can locate meetings through an official AA website or local AA service office for your country or region. Meeting directories may identify newcomer, beginner, women’s, men’s, LGBTQ+, young people’s, language-specific and online meetings. These descriptions can help you find a setting in which you feel comfortable, although availability varies by location.

Try attending different meetings rather than relying on one group. Meeting formats and personalities vary, and a group that does not feel right for you does not represent all of AA. Arrive a little early or stay afterward, when members often have time for informal conversation.

If you are new to the fellowship, you can say:

- “I’m new and hoping to find a sponsor. Is there anyone I could speak with?”
- “How do people usually find sponsors in this group?”
- “Could you introduce me to someone who may be available?”

Some meetings ask members who are willing to sponsor to identify themselves. In others, the chairperson or group members may introduce newcomers privately. There is no single procedure used in every AA group.

If you are still learning how meetings and sponsorship fit together, this [newcomer’s guide to starting recovery with Alcoholics Anonymous](/blog/how-to-start-your-recovery-with-alcoholics-anonymous) explains what to expect.

## Listen before you choose

You do not need to interview every person formally, but it helps to listen carefully. Look for members who speak honestly about both difficulty and growth rather than presenting themselves as experts with all the answers.

A potential sponsor may be a good fit if they:

- Have personal experience of sobriety and working the Twelve Steps
- Participate consistently in AA meetings or service
- Seem honest, stable and willing to admit mistakes
- Focus on AA principles rather than controlling other people
- Can explain how they usually work with a sponsee
- Respect privacy, personal agency and appropriate boundaries
- Encourage connection with a wider recovery community
- Are reasonably available without promising constant access

Length of sobriety can matter, but time alone does not guarantee a healthy fit. Pay attention to how someone treats newcomers, responds to disagreement and speaks about former sponsees. Recovery experience should be accompanied by humility and respect.

For a fuller checklist, explore [what to look for in an AA sponsor](/blog/what-to-look-for-in-an-aa-sponsor).

## Consider personal comfort and safety

You should be able to discuss sensitive experiences with your sponsor without feeling pressured, intimidated or judged. Some AA members prefer a sponsor of the same gender, and this is a common custom in parts of the fellowship, but it is not a universal solution or requirement. Gender, sexuality, culture, age, language, trauma history and personal safety may all influence whom you trust.

Choose someone with whom clear boundaries are possible. You are not required to disclose private information immediately, meet in isolated places or accept unwanted physical, romantic, sexual or financial involvement.

Warning signs include someone who:

- Demands obedience or claims authority over your life
- Pressures you to stop prescribed medication or ignore healthcare advice
- Asks for money, favors or secrecy unrelated to Step work
- Pursues a romantic or sexual relationship through sponsorship
- Tries to separate you from family, friends, treatment or other support
- Uses humiliation, threats or fear to motivate you
- Claims their method is the only legitimate path to sobriety
- Repeatedly ignores your stated boundaries

A sponsor may challenge your thinking or encourage honest action, but sponsorship should not remove your right to make decisions. AA is also not the only route to recovery. Healthcare, counseling, medication where clinically appropriate, mutual-help groups and community services may all form part of a recovery plan.

## How to approach a potential sponsor

Once you have identified someone, speak with them before or after a meeting, call them if they have offered their number, or send a brief private message after an online meeting.

Keep the request simple:

> “I appreciate what you share about recovery. Are you available to sponsor someone at the moment?”

You could also ask:

> “I’m looking for help working the Twelve Steps. Would you be willing to sponsor me, or meet for a conversation about it?”

You do not need to give a speech or prove that you deserve help. Asking is a normal part of AA. The person may want to talk about expectations before answering.

Useful questions include:

- How do you guide someone through the Steps?
- How often do you usually have contact?
- Do you prefer calls, messages or in-person meetings?
- What do you expect from a sponsee?
- What should I do if you are unavailable during a difficult moment?
- Are there boundaries you want us to discuss?

Our guide on [how to ask someone to sponsor you in AA](/blog/how-to-ask-someone-to-sponsor-you) offers additional wording and ways to respond if someone declines.

## What if the person says no?

A refusal is not necessarily personal. Someone may already sponsor several people, have limited time, be dealing with health or family responsibilities, or simply believe another member would suit you better.

Thank them and ask whether they can suggest someone else. Then keep attending meetings and making connections. It may take several conversations to find an available sponsor.

Do not let embarrassment keep you isolated. Asking for support is an act of participation, not failure.

## Can you choose a temporary sponsor?

Yes. Some newcomers ask someone to act as a temporary sponsor while they become familiar with AA and meet more people. This can allow you to start discussing recovery and the Steps without treating the decision as permanent.

Be clear about what “temporary” means to both of you. You might agree to talk for a few weeks and then review whether the arrangement is working. In practice, a temporary relationship may become long-term, or either person may decide that another match would be healthier.

Changing sponsors is allowed. Sponsorship is voluntary, and neither person is obligated to continue an unhelpful relationship. Ideally, communicate directly and respectfully, while ensuring you still have support during any transition.

## Finding a sponsor through online AA meetings

Online sponsorship can be useful if you live far from meetings, travel frequently, have mobility or caregiving limitations, need meetings in a particular language, or feel safer beginning remotely.

Attend the same online meetings regularly so that you can become familiar with members over time. Use the meeting’s recommended method for exchanging contact details, and protect personal information until trust develops.

Before agreeing to online sponsorship, discuss time zones, communication methods, privacy and how often you will speak. Video or telephone conversations may offer more context than text messages alone. If you later meet in person, choose a public setting and tell someone you trust where you are going.

## Do not rely on one person for every need

A sponsor can be important, but a strong recovery network usually includes more than one relationship. Get phone numbers from several members, attend meetings consistently and build connections with people who understand sobriety.

Your sponsor may sometimes be asleep, working, traveling or managing their own life. Knowing several people you can contact reduces pressure on the sponsorship relationship and gives you a broader range of experience.

Professional care may also be important. If you experience severe shaking, confusion, hallucinations, seizures or other alarming symptoms when reducing or stopping alcohol, seek urgent medical help through local emergency services. Abruptly stopping alcohol can be dangerous for some dependent drinkers, so obtain guidance from a qualified healthcare professional rather than relying on a sponsor for detoxification advice.

## Take one practical step today

Finding a sponsor becomes easier when you turn the search into small actions. Today, you could:

1. Find one AA meeting you can attend.
2. Tell one member that you are looking for a sponsor.
3. Listen for someone whose recovery seems grounded and honest.
4. Ask that person whether they have time for a short conversation.
5. Continue attending meetings even if the first answer is no.

You do not need complete certainty before beginning. A sponsor is a fellow member walking beside you through the AA program, not someone who must have a flawless personality or every answer.

## Conclusion

To find an AA sponsor, attend meetings regularly, speak openly about needing help and look for someone with Twelve Step experience, healthy boundaries and a way of living in sobriety that you respect. Ask directly, discuss expectations and remember that the arrangement can change. The aim is not perfection; it is a safe, practical relationship that helps you take honest action in recovery.

## Frequently asked questions

### How soon should I get an AA sponsor?

There is no universal deadline. Many people benefit from finding a sponsor early so they can begin working the Twelve Steps and have regular guidance. You can start asking as soon as you attend meetings, even if you choose a temporary sponsor first.

### Does an AA sponsor need a certain amount of sobriety?

AA has no universal minimum period for sponsorship. Many members look for someone who has been sober longer than they have and has personal experience working the Steps. Consistency, judgment, availability and healthy boundaries matter alongside length of sobriety.

### Can an AA sponsor live in another city or country?

Yes. Sponsorship can take place by telephone, video call or online messaging. Discuss time zones, privacy, contact frequency and what you will do when urgent support is needed. Maintaining local recovery contacts can still be valuable.

### What if I cannot find a sponsor immediately?

Keep attending meetings, tell members you are looking and ask whether someone can provide temporary guidance. Build a list of recovery contacts rather than waiting for one ideal person. You can also use professional and non-AA support while deciding whether AA sponsorship is right for you.
