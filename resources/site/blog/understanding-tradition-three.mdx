Tradition Three of Alcoholics Anonymous answers a basic but important question: who is allowed to belong to AA? Its short-form wording is: “The only requirement for A.A. membership is a desire to stop drinking.”

In practical terms, a person does not have to prove that they have alcohol use disorder, complete an assessment, pay a fee, accept a religious belief or achieve sobriety before becoming an AA member. The stated requirement is simply a desire to stop drinking.

This makes Tradition Three one of AA’s clearest safeguards against gatekeeping. It leaves the decision to seek membership with the individual rather than a committee, professional or established member. At the same time, it does not mean that groups must ignore unsafe or seriously disruptive conduct. Understanding that distinction helps newcomers and experienced members apply the tradition with both compassion and good judgement.

## What is AA Tradition Three?

Alcoholics Anonymous has Twelve Traditions as well as Twelve Steps. The Steps offer a suggested path of personal recovery, while the Traditions help groups protect their unity, purpose and independence.

Tradition Three concerns membership. It establishes a deliberately simple threshold: the desire to stop drinking. AA does not require a medical diagnosis, referral, membership card, application form or particular length of sobriety.

This is especially important because people often arrive at an AA meeting feeling ashamed, uncertain or afraid of being judged. Some have experienced severe consequences from alcohol. Others have not lost a job, relationship, home or health. Tradition Three does not create separate membership standards based on how much someone drank or how visibly alcohol affected their life.

A person does not have to reach a dramatic “rock bottom” before seeking help. If you are questioning your drinking, you may also find it useful to learn [who can join Alcoholics Anonymous and what membership means](/blog/who-can-join-alcoholics-anonymous).

## What does “a desire to stop drinking” mean?

The phrase is intentionally straightforward. It focuses on what a person wants rather than demanding evidence of past failure or future success.

### Desire does not mean perfect certainty

Many newcomers feel conflicted. They may want to stop drinking but still miss alcohol, fear sober life or wonder whether permanent abstinence is possible. That conflict does not automatically cancel the desire to stop.

A desire can be hesitant. It can coexist with cravings, fear and doubt. AA members are not required to arrive with complete confidence or a detailed recovery plan.

However, the wording refers specifically to a desire to stop drinking. Someone who is only curious about AA, supporting another person or studying recovery may be welcome at an open AA meeting, but that is different from claiming AA membership. Local meeting descriptions usually explain who may attend.

### Desire is not the same as achievement

Tradition Three does not say that a person must already be sober. Newcomers may attend while struggling to stop, and a person who returns after drinking again does not have to earn their way back.

AA meetings are intended to support recovery, not reward people for already having mastered it. This matters because shame after a return to drinking can prevent someone from seeking help when connection may be most valuable.

### No one has to prove a diagnosis

AA traditionally uses terms such as “alcoholic,” but Tradition Three does not require a formal diagnosis. Alcohol use disorder is a clinical term assessed by qualified healthcare professionals; AA membership is not a medical diagnosis.

A meeting member should not demand medical records, compare drinking histories or decide that another person did not drink enough to qualify. People may describe themselves differently, particularly when they are new. Listening and allowing them to reach their own understanding is generally more consistent with the inclusive spirit of the tradition.

## Why Tradition Three matters

Without a simple membership principle, groups could begin setting additional conditions. They might exclude people because of age, background, beliefs, finances, mental health, past behavior or the details of their drinking history.

Tradition Three helps prevent that drift. It reminds members that AA’s purpose is not to create a socially selective organization. Its focus is helping people who want to stop drinking.

This connects closely with [Tradition Five and the primary purpose of an AA group](/blog/understanding-tradition-five). When groups concentrate on carrying a recovery message, personal differences become less important than the shared problem and shared hope.

The tradition may be particularly reassuring to people who worry that they:

- are too young or too old for AA;
- have not experienced consequences serious enough to belong;
- do not identify with every story they hear;
- are not religious or are uncertain about spiritual language;
- previously attended and then returned to drinking;
- also use professional treatment, medication or another recovery program;
- do not yet understand the Twelve Steps;
- cannot afford to contribute money.

None of these creates an additional AA membership requirement. AA can also be one part of a broader recovery plan rather than the only source of support.

## Tradition Three and open or closed AA meetings

AA membership and meeting attendance are related, but they are not exactly the same issue. Meetings are commonly described as open or closed, although formats and descriptions can vary by location.

### Open AA meetings

Open meetings generally welcome anyone interested in learning about Alcoholics Anonymous. Attendees may include family members, friends, healthcare students or other visitors. The meeting may set expectations about confidentiality and whether visitors can speak.

### Closed AA meetings

Closed meetings are generally intended for AA members or people who have a desire to stop drinking. This narrower format can give participants a focused space to discuss their own recovery from alcohol problems.

A closed meeting is not meant to create an elite level of membership. It is a meeting format based on the shared purpose of stopping drinking. Someone uncertain about whether a meeting is suitable can check its listing or contact the local group, intergroup or AA service office. The same principle applies to online meetings.

People who want a broader introduction can review [what Alcoholics Anonymous is and how AA meetings work](/blog/what-is-alcoholics-anonymous-and-how-does-it-work).

## Does Tradition Three mean there are no boundaries?

No. Inclusive membership does not require a group to tolerate threats, harassment, predatory behavior, discrimination directed at participants or repeated serious disruption.

A group may use its group conscience—the collective decision-making process of its members—to establish practical meeting boundaries. Depending on the situation, members might speak privately with someone, restate meeting expectations, involve a venue representative or ask a person to leave a particular session. Immediate danger should be handled by contacting local emergency services or other appropriate local support.

There is an important distinction between addressing conduct and declaring that someone is permanently unworthy of recovery. A group can take reasonable steps to protect participants without creating extra membership tests based on identity, popularity or personal history.

Boundaries should focus on observable behavior and safety rather than rumor or dislike. Groups can also consider the needs of vulnerable newcomers, provide clear points of contact and review how concerns are handled.

Tradition Three is therefore not a demand for passivity. Its challenge is to combine an open door to recovery with responsible care for the meeting environment.

## Common misunderstandings about Tradition Three

### “You must call yourself an alcoholic”

Many AA members introduce themselves that way, but the short-form Tradition Three does not state that a person must use a particular label. Meeting customs vary, and newcomers may need time to understand their relationship with alcohol.

### “You must believe in God before joining”

Tradition Three contains no religious test. AA’s Steps include spiritual ideas and reference a Higher Power, but members interpret spiritual language in different ways. Belief does not have to be settled before attending or belonging.

### “You must complete the Steps to remain a member”

The Twelve Steps are AA’s suggested recovery program, but Tradition Three does not make Step completion a membership condition. Members often work through the Steps with a sponsor, yet there is no entrance examination or graduation requirement.

### “Drinking again means you are expelled”

A return to drinking may be serious and may indicate that more support is needed, but it does not erase the possibility of coming back. Tradition Three places the emphasis on the continuing desire to stop.

### “Anyone can attend every meeting for any reason”

This confuses membership with meeting format. Open meetings may welcome the wider public, while closed meetings are intended for those with a desire to stop drinking. Groups may also use specialized formats while applying AA’s wider traditions and local group conscience.

## How AA groups can put Tradition Three into practice

Tradition Three becomes meaningful through ordinary group behavior. Members and trusted servants—AA’s term for people carrying out service roles—can support it by:

1. **Welcoming newcomers without interrogation.** Offer practical information rather than demanding a detailed drinking history.
2. **Explaining meeting formats clearly.** State whether a meeting is open or closed and describe participation expectations respectfully.
3. **Avoiding comparisons.** Consequences, drinking patterns and recovery histories differ. Competitive storytelling can make people feel that they do not qualify.
4. **Making room for uncertainty.** A newcomer may listen for several meetings before feeling comfortable speaking or using AA language.
5. **Separating behavior from identity.** Address harmful conduct directly without turning personal dislike into exclusion.
6. **Protecting confidentiality.** Members should not reveal another person’s attendance or personal story without permission.
7. **Offering additional options.** Someone may benefit from another meeting, professional care, peer support or a different recovery pathway.

No single member owns an AA group or has authority over AA membership as a whole. Group decisions are normally made through group conscience rather than personal status or length of sobriety.

## What Tradition Three means for a newcomer

If you want to stop drinking, Tradition Three says you do not need to wait until you feel worthy, certain or fully sober. You can look for a suitable meeting and begin listening.

You might take these simple steps:

- Find an open or closed AA meeting that matches your situation.
- Contact the meeting organizer if the listing is unclear.
- Arrive a few minutes early and say that you are new, if you feel comfortable.
- Listen for shared experience rather than expecting every story to match yours.
- Try more than one group, since meeting styles and cultures vary.
- Speak with a healthcare professional if you are concerned about withdrawal, physical health or mental health.

Abruptly stopping alcohol can be medically dangerous for some people who have become physically dependent. AA meetings do not replace medical assessment or supervised withdrawal care. Seek guidance from a qualified healthcare professional or local alcohol and drug service if you may be at risk. Contact local emergency services for severe symptoms or immediate danger.

## Conclusion

Tradition Three keeps the entrance to Alcoholics Anonymous simple: the only membership requirement is a desire to stop drinking. It rejects tests based on status, diagnosis, belief, money or past success while leaving room for groups to maintain safe, focused meetings.

For newcomers, its message is hopeful. You do not need a perfect recovery record before seeking recovery support. A willingness to begin is enough to approach the door.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I have to be sober before I can join AA?

No. Tradition Three does not require existing sobriety. People often come to AA because they are struggling to stop. If you may be physically dependent on alcohol, obtain professional medical guidance before stopping abruptly.

### Can I attend AA if I am not sure that I am an alcoholic?

You can generally attend an open AA meeting to learn more. Closed meetings are usually for people who identify as AA members or have a desire to stop drinking. Check the local meeting description if you are uncertain.

### Can an AA group ask someone to leave a meeting?

A group may respond to unsafe or seriously disruptive behavior and may ask someone to leave a particular meeting. That is different from imposing additional identity-based membership requirements or declaring that the person can never seek recovery through AA.

### Does Tradition Three mean AA is the right choice for everyone?

No. Tradition Three explains who may belong; it does not claim that AA suits every person. Recovery may also involve professional treatment, medication, counseling, secular peer support, faith-based help or other community resources. People can use AA alongside other appropriate support.
