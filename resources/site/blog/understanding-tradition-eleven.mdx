Tradition Eleven helps Alcoholics Anonymous communicate with the public without turning recovery into a publicity campaign. Its central ideas are **attraction rather than promotion** and **personal anonymity in public media**.

In practical terms, AA can make accurate information available, help people find an AA meeting and explain how its program works. However, members avoid advertising AA through pressure, exaggerated promises or personal celebrity. They also protect their identity as AA members at public-facing levels such as news media, broadcasting, films and widely accessible online platforms.

This tradition is not about hiding recovery or making AA secret. It is about allowing the message to stand on its own while protecting members, the Fellowship and the person who may still be deciding whether to seek help.

## What does AA Tradition Eleven say?

The short form of Tradition Eleven states:

> “Our public relations policy is based on attraction rather than promotion; we need always maintain personal anonymity at the level of press, radio and films.”

The Twelve Traditions are principles intended to support the unity and continued functioning of AA groups. While the Twelve Steps focus mainly on individual recovery, the Traditions address how groups and members relate to one another, AA as a whole and the wider public.

For anyone unfamiliar with the Fellowship, this overview of [what Alcoholics Anonymous is and how AA works](/blog/what-is-alcoholics-anonymous-and-how-does-it-work) explains meetings, sponsorship and the Twelve Steps.

Tradition Eleven has two closely connected parts:

1. AA communicates through attraction, not promotion.
2. Members maintain personal anonymity when identifying themselves publicly as AA members.

## What does “attraction rather than promotion” mean?

Attraction means making AA visible, available and understandable without trying to sell it.

People may be attracted to AA when they encounter members whose lives have improved through sobriety, hear an honest account of recovery or receive clear information about local meetings. The focus remains on what the program offers rather than on persuading someone that they must join.

Promotion can include:

- Making inflated promises about what AA will achieve
- Presenting AA as guaranteed to work for everyone
- Pressuring people to attend
- Using celebrity endorsement to strengthen AA’s reputation
- Competing with or criticizing other recovery approaches
- Seeking publicity for personal recognition

Attraction does not mean remaining silent. AA groups and service bodies can provide meeting information, answer questions from professionals, cooperate with community organizations and explain AA’s purpose. Members may also carry the message through personal contact and service, an idea explored further in [Step Twelve and carrying the AA message](/blog/understanding-step-twelve-of-alcoholics-anonymous).

The difference is largely one of tone and purpose. Information says, “This help is available.” Promotion says, “You should choose us, and we can promise a particular result.”

AA is one possible source of peer support, not the only route to recovery. Some people also use professional treatment, medication, counseling, secular support groups, faith communities or other recovery programs.

## Why does Tradition Eleven protect public anonymity?

Public anonymity helps prevent one person from becoming the public face of Alcoholics Anonymous.

If a well-known or highly visible member publicly identifies as an AA member, audiences may begin to judge the Fellowship through that individual’s behavior. If the person later drinks, becomes involved in controversy or makes unsupported claims, AA’s wider message could be affected.

Anonymity also reduces the risk of status and personality overshadowing recovery principles. A famous member’s experience is not more authoritative than anyone else’s, and no individual member speaks for all of AA.

Public anonymity can also provide reassurance to newcomers. People may be more willing to enter an AA meeting when they understand that membership is not intended to become a source of public exposure.

This does not mean members must conceal sobriety from family, friends or healthcare professionals. Individuals can decide what to share about their own recovery in private life. The public boundary becomes especially relevant when someone uses their full identity, image or recognizable public profile while explicitly presenting themselves as an AA member.

Members should never reveal another person’s AA membership without permission. Even with permission, public identification deserves particular care because the consequences may be difficult to reverse.

## How does Tradition Eleven apply to social media?

Tradition Eleven was written before social media, video platforms, podcasts and personal websites. Its principle nevertheless provides a useful way to think about modern public communication.

A private conversation or restricted recovery group is different from a public post that can be copied, searched and shared. Privacy settings can change, screenshots can circulate, and content intended for a small audience may reach thousands of people.

Before posting, members can ask:

- Is this account or page accessible to the general public?
- Am I identifying myself by full name or a clearly recognizable image?
- Am I explicitly presenting myself as an AA member?
- Does this post identify anyone else as attending AA?
- Could my wording appear to speak for AA as a whole?
- Am I sharing experience, or promoting myself through AA membership?

A person can talk publicly about sobriety or recovery without necessarily announcing AA membership. If discussing AA in public, it is possible to focus on general information while avoiding identifying individual members.

Photos taken around meetings require special care. A group photograph, tagged location or casual background image may reveal attendance even when nobody is named. Asking permission is important, but avoiding public meeting photographs altogether may offer stronger protection.

## Practical ways to follow Tradition Eleven

Tradition Eleven is usually practiced through thoughtful choices rather than rigid policing. Situations differ, and members can consult experienced AA service members or local service guidance when uncertain.

### When speaking about your recovery

You can share your experience honestly without claiming that your result is universal. Use phrases such as “This helped me” rather than “This will work for everyone.”

Avoid presenting yourself as an official AA spokesperson unless you have been specifically asked to perform an appropriate service role. Even then, personal anonymity remains relevant in public media.

### When sharing meeting information

Provide practical facts such as time, format and accessibility. Avoid promising that a particular meeting will keep someone sober or solve every problem.

Remember that AA meetings may vary in size, tone and format. One meeting does not represent every AA group.

### When posting online

Review the audience, privacy settings and identifying details. Do not tag people in recovery-related content without their clear agreement, and do not post attendance lists, screenshots or recognizable meeting images.

If you regret a post, remove it where possible and ask others not to distribute it. Complete removal cannot always be guaranteed, which is why pausing before posting is valuable.

### When approached by media

Do not assume that enthusiasm alone makes you responsible for representing AA. Consider contacting the relevant local AA service structure for guidance. An interview may sometimes proceed using anonymity safeguards, but the details should be considered carefully before recording or publication.

## Tradition Eleven and Tradition Twelve

Traditions Eleven and Twelve both discuss anonymity, but their emphasis differs.

Tradition Eleven focuses specifically on personal anonymity in public communication and AA’s approach to public relations. Tradition Twelve describes anonymity as the spiritual foundation of all the Traditions and emphasizes placing principles before personalities.

Together, they discourage fame, status and personal influence from becoming more important than AA’s shared purpose. A fuller guide to [Tradition Twelve and principles before personalities](/blog/understanding-tradition-twelve) explains that broader spiritual meaning.

## Common misunderstandings about Tradition Eleven

### “AA is not allowed to communicate with the public”

Tradition Eleven does not require silence. AA can make meeting information available, respond to public questions and explain what it does. The distinction is between providing information and aggressively marketing the Fellowship.

### “I can never tell anyone I attend AA”

Personal disclosure in private life is generally different from publicly identifying yourself as an AA member through mass media or an open online platform. Members make their own decisions about sharing with trusted people while respecting everyone else’s privacy.

### “Anonymity means using secrecy to avoid accountability”

Anonymity should not be used to excuse harmful conduct or prevent someone from seeking help. Safety, legal responsibilities and appropriate accountability still matter. AA confidentiality is also not necessarily the same as legally protected confidentiality offered by certain qualified professionals.

### “Tradition Eleven prevents people from discussing other recovery options”

AA does not have to be presented as the only answer. Respecting other forms of support is consistent with avoiding promotional claims. People can combine AA participation with healthcare, counseling or other appropriate recovery resources.

## What Tradition Eleven means for newcomers

A newcomer does not need to master the Traditions before attending a meeting. Tradition Eleven mainly offers reassurance: you should not be used as publicity, and other members should not publicly disclose your attendance.

You can usually introduce yourself by first name only, listen without speaking and ask how that group handles anonymity. Online and in-person meeting formats differ, so check the meeting information and protect identifying details according to your comfort level.

If you may be physically dependent on alcohol, seek guidance from a qualified healthcare professional before stopping abruptly. Alcohol withdrawal can be dangerous for some people, and AA is peer support rather than medical detoxification or emergency care.

## Conclusion

Tradition Eleven allows AA to be visible without becoming promotional. Attraction comes from honest recovery, useful information and respectful service—not pressure, publicity or personalities.

By maintaining public anonymity and protecting other members, people help preserve equality, trust and space for the AA message itself.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does Tradition Eleven mean AA cannot advertise meetings?

AA groups can make meeting information publicly available and explain the Fellowship’s purpose. The principle discourages sales-style promotion, competition, exaggerated claims and pressure rather than straightforward public information.

### Can I say publicly that I am sober?

Yes. Sobriety is not exclusive to AA, and discussing your recovery does not automatically identify you as an AA member. Tradition Eleven becomes particularly relevant when a person publicly connects their full identity or recognizable image with AA membership.

### Can I post a photograph from an AA meeting?

It is safer not to post recognizable meeting photographs publicly. An image may identify members, reveal a location or expose attendance unintentionally. Permission does not always remove the wider risk because online material can be copied and redistributed.

### Is Tradition Eleven the same as keeping everything said in a meeting confidential?

Not exactly. Tradition Eleven concerns attraction rather than promotion and personal anonymity in public media. Respecting what others share in meetings is an important privacy practice, but it is not identical to professional or legally protected confidentiality. If you are unsure about a group’s expectations, ask the chairperson or another trusted member.
