Tradition Four of Alcoholics Anonymous gives each AA group considerable freedom to decide how it operates. At the same time, it asks the group to consider whether its decisions could harm other groups or AA as a whole.

In simple terms, an AA group can shape its own meetings, service roles and local practices without needing permission from a central authority. However, autonomy is not complete isolation. Freedom comes with responsibility to AA’s unity, reputation and primary purpose.

For newcomers, this helps explain why two AA meetings may feel very different while still belonging to the same worldwide fellowship. One meeting may focus on discussion, another on a speaker, and another on studying AA literature. Tradition Four allows that variety while providing an important boundary: a group’s choices should not create problems for the wider fellowship.

## What does AA Tradition Four say?

The short form of Tradition Four states:

> “Each group should be autonomous except in matters affecting other groups or A.A. as a whole.”

The word **autonomous** means self-governing. An AA group generally makes its own decisions rather than receiving instructions from leaders at a regional, national or international level.

This reflects the broader structure of Alcoholics Anonymous. AA does not operate like a company, government agency or treatment provider with a central management team controlling every local meeting. Service offices and committees support communication and shared work, but individual groups retain substantial independence.

If you are unfamiliar with this structure, our guide to [what Alcoholics Anonymous is and how AA works](/blog/what-is-alcoholics-anonymous-and-how-does-it-work) explains meetings, membership, sponsorship and the Twelve Steps.

## Why is group autonomy important in AA?

AA groups meet in different cultures, languages and communities. They may gather in community centers, places of worship, treatment settings, correctional facilities or online. A meeting that works well in a large city may not suit a small rural community or an international online group.

Tradition Four allows groups to respond to their local circumstances. For example, a group may decide:

- When and where to meet
- Whether the meeting is open or closed
- Whether to use a discussion, speaker or literature-study format
- How long the meeting lasts
- How service positions are filled
- How often business meetings take place
- How to welcome newcomers
- How to manage online access or meeting security
- How group funds are used for legitimate AA purposes

These decisions are usually made through a **group conscience**. This is an informed collective decision reached after members have had an opportunity to discuss the issue. It is not necessarily a simple contest in which the largest side wins. Ideally, members listen carefully, consider AA principles and make room for minority views.

Autonomy gives groups room to learn through experience. A group can try a format, review whether it helps, and change course when necessary. It does not have to wait for instructions from elsewhere.

## What are the limits of AA group autonomy?

The second half of Tradition Four is as important as the first. A group’s autonomy has limits when its actions affect another AA group or Alcoholics Anonymous as a whole.

A local decision may become a wider concern when it:

- Creates confusion about what AA is or does
- Suggests that AA endorses an outside organization, political cause or product
- Interferes with another group’s meeting or membership
- Risks members’ anonymity beyond the group level
- Uses the AA name in a misleading way
- Distracts from AA’s purpose of carrying its recovery message
- Creates financial or legal obligations for other AA groups or service bodies
- Publicly presents one group’s opinion as the position of all Alcoholics Anonymous

For example, a group is free to choose its own meeting format. It would be different if the group announced that its format was the only form of “real AA” and tried to impose it on neighboring groups.

Similarly, a group may cooperate practically with the facility where it meets, but it should be cautious about implying that AA endorses that facility or its services. This connects with [Tradition Six and the protection of recovery from outside interests](/blog/understanding-tradition-six).

Tradition Four therefore does not mean, “Our group can do anything it wants.” A more accurate interpretation is, “Our group can manage its own affairs while considering the wellbeing of AA beyond itself.”

## Autonomy does not mean separation

An autonomous AA group can still participate fully in the wider fellowship. It may send a representative to local service meetings, contribute to shared AA services, exchange information with nearby groups and take part in events involving multiple groups.

These relationships do not remove autonomy. They allow independent groups to cooperate voluntarily.

This balance can seem unusual. AA groups are independent, yet interconnected. No group has to become identical to another, but each benefits from preserving a recognizable common purpose and shared set of principles.

Tradition Four also works alongside Tradition Nine, which explains why AA uses service boards and committees without creating a governing hierarchy. Our article on [service without hierarchy in Tradition Nine](/blog/understanding-tradition-nine) explores that structure in more detail.

## How group conscience supports Tradition Four

A healthy group conscience helps prevent autonomy from becoming the personal authority of one influential member. Decisions affecting the group should not normally be made solely by the chairperson, secretary, longest-sober member or most confident speaker.

Before making an important decision, a group may find it helpful to:

1. **Define the question clearly.** Members should understand what is being proposed and why.
2. **Gather relevant information.** This might include the experience of other groups or guidance from AA service resources.
3. **Review the Traditions.** Several Traditions may apply to the same issue.
4. **Invite broad participation.** Quieter members and minority views should have space to be heard.
5. **Allow time when possible.** Not every decision needs to be made immediately.
6. **Consider the wider effect.** Ask whether the choice may affect neighboring groups, local services or public understanding of AA.
7. **Review the outcome later.** Group decisions can be reconsidered if experience reveals an unintended problem.

A group conscience is not guaranteed to produce a perfect answer. Members may disagree, and different groups may reach different reasonable decisions. Tradition Four allows for this variation while encouraging humility and accountability.

## Practical examples of Tradition Four

### Choosing a meeting format

One group decides to devote its weekly meeting to a reading followed by member sharing. Another uses a speaker format. Both choices can fit within Tradition Four because each group is shaping its own meeting without dictating what others must do.

### Setting local meeting customs

A group may decide how members introduce themselves, how sharing time is managed or whether refreshments are available. Customs are not automatically universal AA rules. Newcomers should not assume that one group’s practice applies everywhere.

### Responding to disruptive behavior

A group may develop safety practices for disruptive or threatening conduct. Autonomy allows it to respond to its circumstances, but decisions should be made thoughtfully rather than used to control, shame or punish members. Immediate safety concerns may require contacting venue staff, local emergency services or other appropriate local support.

### Holding an online meeting

An online AA group may choose its platform, access controls and approach to disruptive participants. It should also consider privacy, anonymity and whether publicly shared links expose members or affect other groups.

### Supporting an outside cause

Individual AA members are free to hold personal political, religious and social views. An AA group, however, should be careful about publicly supporting an outside campaign in AA’s name. Doing so could imply that the entire fellowship shares that position.

## Tradition Four for individual members

The Twelve Traditions primarily guide AA groups and the fellowship, not the private lives of members. Tradition Four should not be used to tell someone how to manage employment, relationships, healthcare or personal beliefs.

Even so, members sometimes find its underlying balance useful: make responsible choices while considering how those choices affect others. This is a personal application, not an official rule derived from the Tradition.

It is also important not to confuse group autonomy with personal permission to disregard meeting boundaries. A member may disagree with a group decision, but one person’s preference does not automatically override the group conscience.

If disagreement continues, the member might calmly raise the issue at a business meeting, speak with trusted members, attend another AA meeting or help start a new meeting using appropriate local guidance. Variety among groups means people can often find a setting that better supports their recovery.

## Common misunderstandings about Tradition Four

### “Every meeting can redefine AA however it wants”

Groups have broad freedom, but the AA name carries a wider meaning. The Traditions ask groups to consider AA’s shared purpose, unity and public reputation.

### “Service committees control local groups”

AA service bodies can share collective experience and provide support, but they do not ordinarily govern groups. Groups choose whether and how to participate in the service structure.

### “All AA meetings should operate in the same way”

Tradition Four makes local variation possible. Differences do not necessarily indicate that one group is right and another is wrong.

### “Autonomy means a group never needs outside input”

A group can remain autonomous while seeking experience from other groups, service representatives or AA resources. Listening to guidance is not the same as surrendering responsibility.

## Questions a group can ask before making a decision

When a proposal raises concerns under Tradition Four, these questions may help:

- Does this support the group’s recovery purpose?
- Could it interfere with another AA group?
- Are we speaking only for our group, or appearing to speak for all AA?
- Could this create an endorsement of an outside organization or cause?
- Have we considered anonymity, safety and accessibility?
- Have members received enough information to form an informed group conscience?
- Are personalities, fear or urgency driving the decision?
- Would consulting wider AA experience be useful?

The aim is not to make groups afraid of acting. It is to combine flexibility with care.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the main idea of AA Tradition Four?

Tradition Four gives each AA group freedom to manage its own affairs. That freedom is limited when a decision could affect other groups or Alcoholics Anonymous as a whole.

### Can every AA group create its own rules?

A group can establish local practices through its group conscience, but these are not automatically rules for other meetings. Groups should also consider all Twelve Traditions and avoid actions that misrepresent or harm the wider fellowship.

### Who decides whether a group’s action affects AA as a whole?

There is no single AA authority that routinely approves every group decision. Groups are encouraged to use an informed group conscience, consult relevant AA experience and communicate with appropriate local or wider service bodies when an issue has broader consequences.

### Why do AA meetings differ from one another?

Meetings differ because groups are autonomous and serve different communities. Formats, customs and styles may vary, while groups remain connected through AA’s common purpose and principles.

## Conclusion

Tradition Four protects both freedom and unity. It allows AA groups to adapt to local needs, experiment and take responsibility for their own meetings. At the same time, it reminds them that their choices may have consequences beyond the room.

Healthy autonomy is not isolation or unlimited license. It is self-government practiced with humility, group conscience and consideration for other groups and AA as a whole.
