Reading the Alcoholics Anonymous Big Book with a sponsor usually means working through the text together, discussing how it relates to your experience, and turning its ideas into practical Twelve Step action. You do not need to understand every passage immediately. The aim is not to pass a test or agree with every word; it is to read honestly, ask questions and explore how the program might support your sobriety.

A sponsor is an AA member who shares their experience of recovery and helps another member work through the Twelve Steps. Sponsors are not professional counselors, medical providers or authorities over your life. A healthy sponsor helps you engage with the book while respecting your questions, boundaries and personal decisions.

## What is the AA Big Book?

The book commonly called the “Big Book” is *Alcoholics Anonymous*, the foundational text associated with the AA program. It describes alcoholism from the perspective of AA’s early members, presents the Twelve Steps, and includes personal recovery stories.

The language reflects the period in which the book was first published. Some readers find parts of it immediately relatable, while others struggle with older expressions, gendered wording or spiritual language. Reading with a sponsor can help you place those passages in context without pretending that every sentence will feel natural to you.

Different editions and translations may have different page numbers, introductions and personal stories. For that reason, it is often easier to agree on a chapter or section rather than relying only on page numbers.

The Big Book is important within AA, but it is not a medical textbook or a substitute for healthcare. AA is also not the only route to recovery. People may combine meetings and sponsorship with therapy, medication, rehabilitation, peer support or other recovery programs.

## Why read the Big Book with a sponsor?

You can read the book independently, in a study group or at an AA meeting. Working through it with a sponsor adds a personal conversation with someone who has applied its principles in their own life.

A sponsor may help you:

- Understand unfamiliar AA language
- Notice passages connected to each Step
- Separate spiritual principles from rigid interpretations
- Compare the text with your own drinking and recovery experience
- Move from reading into practical action
- Talk through resistance, confusion or fear
- Avoid rushing into sensitive Step work without support

Sponsorship is based on shared experience rather than professional expertise. If you are unfamiliar with the role, this guide to [what an AA sponsor actually does](/blog/what-does-an-aa-sponsor-actually-do) explains both its purpose and its limits.

## Agree on a simple reading plan

There is no single official schedule that every sponsor and sponsee must follow. Some pairs meet weekly and read a few pages at a time. Others assign a chapter between conversations or focus first on the sections that explain the Steps.

Before beginning, ask practical questions such as:

- Which edition or translation will we use?
- What section should I read before our next conversation?
- Should I underline passages or make notes?
- How often will we meet or speak?
- Will we read aloud together or discuss what I read alone?
- How does this reading connect with Step work?
- What should I do if a section troubles or confuses me?

Choose a pace you can maintain. Reading a small section carefully is often more useful than racing through several chapters and remembering little. Consistency matters more than speed.

If you are still establishing communication expectations, consider discussing [how often to speak with an AA sponsor](/blog/how-often-should-you-speak-to-your-sponsor). Contact arrangements should be clear and realistic for both people.

## A practical way to read each section

### 1. Read once for the main idea

Begin by reading the agreed section without trying to analyze every sentence. Ask yourself what the passage seems to be saying about alcohol problems, recovery, relationships, spirituality or action.

If the language feels dated, look for the principle beneath the wording. You can disagree with an expression while still considering whether the broader idea is useful.

### 2. Mark what stands out

You might use a pencil, removable notes or a separate notebook. Mark passages that:

- Describe something you recognize in yourself
- Give you hope
- Create resistance or discomfort
- Seem connected to a Step
- Raise a question
- Suggest an action you could take

There is no need to highlight everything your sponsor highlights. Their experience may guide you, but your own honest response also matters.

### 3. Write brief reflections

A few sentences are enough. Useful prompts include:

- What did I identify with?
- Where was my experience different?
- What did I find difficult to accept?
- What might this section ask me to do?
- Does it change how I understand my drinking?
- What do I want to ask my sponsor?

Writing can slow down automatic reactions and make the later conversation more focused.

### 4. Discuss rather than perform

You do not need to provide the answer you think your sponsor wants. Say when something makes sense, when it does not, and when you are unsure.

A productive discussion might sound like:

- “How did you understand this when you were new?”
- “How does this relate to the Step we are working on?”
- “Is this your experience, or is it stated in the text?”
- “What action did you take after reading this?”
- “Can we talk about the spiritual language here?”
- “I do not identify with this example. Is there another way to understand it?”

A sponsor can offer experience, not the only possible interpretation. AA members vary in their understanding and practice of the program.

### 5. End with one practical action

Reading becomes Step work when ideas influence what you do. Depending on the section and your stage of recovery, an action might be attending an AA meeting, calling another member, writing an inventory, reflecting on willingness or completing an agreed exercise.

The action should be relevant, safe and manageable. Major decisions involving healthcare, finances, legal matters, employment or family safety require appropriate professional guidance, not only sponsorship.

## Connecting the book to the Twelve Steps

Many sponsors use the Big Book as a guide to working the Twelve Steps rather than treating it as a general reading project. The Steps move from acknowledging the problem and becoming open to help, through personal inventory and amends, toward daily reflection, spiritual practice and service.

Newcomers can benefit from an overview of [the Twelve Steps explained simply](/blog/the-twelve-steps-of-alcoholics-anonymous-explained) before exploring individual passages in depth.

Your sponsor may ask you to compare the text with specific experiences from your life. For example, while discussing Step One, you might consider ways alcohol affected your choices or made life difficult to manage. During later Steps, the reading may lead to written work or carefully planned conversations.

Do not make amends, disclose sensitive information or confront someone merely because a passage appears to suggest immediate action. These parts of the program deserve thoughtful discussion, especially where there is a risk of harm to you or another person.

## Handling spiritual or religious language

The Big Book contains frequent references to God and a Higher Power. Some readers welcome this language; others feel uncertain, skeptical or excluded by it.

You do not have to pretend to hold beliefs you do not have. Ask your sponsor how they approached spiritual language and whether their understanding changed over time. In AA, members express spirituality in many ways, and a Higher Power is not necessarily understood through one religion.

You might initially interpret spiritual ideas as openness to help beyond your isolated thinking, trust in the AA fellowship, connection with nature, ethical principles or another concept that feels honest. A good discussion allows room for uncertainty rather than pressuring you into a declaration of belief.

## What healthy Big Book study looks like

Healthy study with a sponsor generally includes curiosity, mutual respect and appropriate boundaries. You should be able to ask questions and make your own decisions.

Be cautious if a sponsor:

- Claims there is only one acceptable interpretation
- Uses the book to control your relationships, money or healthcare
- Demands personal information unrelated to Step work
- Discourages professional treatment or prescribed medication
- Uses fear, shame, threats or humiliation
- Ignores your stated boundaries
- Treats disagreement as proof that you cannot recover

You are allowed to pause a discussion, seek another perspective or change sponsors. Sponsorship is voluntary, and a sponsor should not replace qualified medical, mental health, legal or safeguarding support.

## Keep medical safety separate from book study

Reading the Big Book and attending AA can support recovery, but they do not assess or manage alcohol withdrawal. Abruptly stopping alcohol can be dangerous for some people who have developed physical dependence.

If you drink heavily or regularly, have experienced withdrawal before, or are concerned about symptoms, seek guidance from a qualified healthcare professional or local alcohol and drug service. For severe symptoms or immediate danger, contact local emergency services. A sponsor may encourage you to seek help, but should not provide detoxification instructions.

## Make the reading habit sustainable

Keep your routine uncomplicated. Store your book and notebook somewhere accessible, schedule reading at a relatively calm time, and complete small assignments before they accumulate.

If concentration is difficult in early sobriety, tell your sponsor. You might read one or two pages, listen to an authorized audio edition while following the text, or divide a chapter across several days. Difficulty concentrating is not a moral failure.

It can also help to revisit passages later. Words that initially seem irrelevant may become clearer after more time sober or after working another Step. The goal is not to finish the book once, but to develop principles you can return to in everyday recovery.

## Conclusion

To read the Big Book with a sponsor, agree on a manageable plan, mark what stands out, bring honest questions and connect each discussion with practical Twelve Step action. Your sponsor’s experience can be valuable without becoming unquestionable authority.

Go at a steady pace and remain open about confusion, disagreement or concern. The most useful reading is not necessarily the fastest; it is the reading that helps you build a safer, more honest and sustainable recovery.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does it take to read the Big Book with a sponsor?

There is no standard timeframe. Some people work through key sections over several weeks, while others take months. The pace depends on meeting frequency, reading ability, Step work and current recovery needs. Agree on a realistic rhythm rather than comparing yourself with others.

### Should my sponsor and I read every page together?

Not necessarily. Some sponsors read aloud with sponsees, while others assign sections and discuss them later. You may focus first on the chapters most closely connected to the Twelve Steps before reading the personal stories or other material.

### What if I disagree with something in the Big Book?

Say so respectfully and explore why. Your reaction may lead to a useful discussion about language, context or personal experience. You are not required to abandon critical thinking. Consider the underlying principle and ask how other AA members understand it.

### Can I work the Steps without reading the Big Book with a sponsor?

People participate in AA in different ways, and not everyone follows the same study method. However, many members find that a sponsor helps them understand the text and translate the Steps into action. You can also use meetings, study groups and other recovery resources while deciding what support fits your needs.
