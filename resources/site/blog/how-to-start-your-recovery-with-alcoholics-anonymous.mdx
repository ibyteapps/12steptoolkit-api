Starting recovery with Alcoholics Anonymous can be as simple as finding a meeting and turning up. You do not need to understand the Twelve Steps, introduce yourself as an alcoholic or promise lifelong sobriety before you attend. You can listen, speak if you wish and decide whether the support feels useful.

Alcoholics Anonymous, usually shortened to AA, is a worldwide fellowship of people who share their experience of recovering from alcohol problems. Its approach centres on mutual support, regular meetings and the Twelve Steps. AA is free to attend, apart from optional contributions towards meeting expenses, and you do not need a professional referral.

AA is one route into recovery, not the only one. Some people combine it with support from a GP, alcohol service, counsellor, rehabilitation programme or another mutual-aid group. The important first step is to seek safe, appropriate support rather than trying to manage everything alone.

## Put safety first when stopping alcohol

If you drink heavily or regularly, or have previously experienced withdrawal, seek professional guidance before abruptly stopping. Alcohol withdrawal can be dangerous for some dependent drinkers, and an AA meeting is not a substitute for medical assessment or supervised treatment.

Contact your GP, NHS 111 or a local alcohol service for advice. In an emergency or if severe symptoms develop, call 999. You can still explore AA while arranging medical support; the two can work alongside each other.

If you are preparing for an alcohol-free day, this guide to [your first 24 hours without alcohol](/blog/your-first-24-hours-without-alcohol) offers practical safety and support considerations.

## Find your first AA meeting

AA meetings take place in community halls, religious buildings and other local venues, as well as online. You can use the official Alcoholics Anonymous meeting finder for your country or contact a local AA helpline to ask what is available nearby.

Meeting listings may contain unfamiliar terms. The most common are:

- **Open meeting:** Anyone interested in AA may attend, including relatives, professionals and people who are unsure whether they have an alcohol problem.
- **Closed meeting:** Intended for people who identify as having a problem with alcohol or have a desire to stop drinking.
- **Discussion meeting:** Members share their experiences around a recovery topic.
- **Speaker meeting:** One or more members tell their recovery story.
- **Step meeting:** The group discusses one of the Twelve Steps.
- **Online meeting:** A meeting held by video call or, in some cases, telephone.

For a first visit, look for a meeting described as newcomer-friendly, beginners’ or open. However, an ordinary local meeting can also be a perfectly suitable starting point.

If attending in person feels overwhelming, an online meeting may be a gentler introduction. You could also ask someone you trust to travel to the venue with you, although they may only be able to enter if it is an open meeting.

## What to expect at your first AA meeting

Most AA meetings are informal. Chairs are usually arranged in a circle or around tables, and a group member opens and guides the meeting. There may be brief readings about AA’s programme, followed by members sharing their personal experience.

People generally speak one at a time without interruption. The emphasis is on what helped them rather than giving instructions to others. Meetings vary in atmosphere and format, so one meeting cannot represent the whole of AA.

You may be invited to introduce yourself by your first name. You can simply say, “I’m new and I’m here to listen.” You do not have to tell your story, describe how much you drink or accept a particular label.

AA places importance on anonymity. Members are expected not to identify other attendees outside the meeting or repeat personal stories in a way that reveals who shared them. As in any group setting, you can still protect your privacy by sharing only what feels appropriate.

There is no attendance charge. A collection may be made to cover costs such as room hire and refreshments, but contributing is voluntary.

## Focus on listening rather than understanding everything

Your first meeting may include unfamiliar language, spiritual ideas or stories that do not closely resemble your own. You do not need to agree with or understand everything immediately.

Listen for similarities rather than differences. Notice whether people describe feelings, consequences or patterns you recognise. Pay attention to practical actions that helped them avoid the next drink.

It is also reasonable to have questions or reservations. AA groups are autonomous, meaning each group manages its own affairs within the wider AA traditions. Personalities and meeting styles therefore differ. If your first meeting does not suit you, try another before deciding whether AA has anything to offer.

## Introduce yourself to another member

Recovery often becomes more manageable when it moves from a private intention to a supported plan. Before or after the meeting, consider telling someone that you are new.

You could ask:

- Are there other beginner-friendly meetings nearby?
- What helped you during your first few days?
- Is there someone I could call if I feel like drinking?
- How do sponsorship and the Twelve Steps work?

Members may offer telephone numbers so you can contact them between meetings. Using those numbers can feel awkward at first, but contact between meetings is a normal part of AA support. You do not need to wait until a crisis. A brief call or message can help you feel connected and interrupt an urge to drink.

Keep sensible personal boundaries. AA members provide peer support, not professional treatment, medical care, financial assistance or emergency services.

## Learn what a sponsor does

A sponsor is an AA member who supports another person in working through the Twelve Steps. They usually share what they did in recovery, answer questions about AA and offer regular one-to-one contact.

Sponsorship is not clinical therapy, medical supervision or a controlling relationship. A sponsor should not make decisions about your medication, finances, employment or personal safety.

You do not have to choose a sponsor at your first meeting. Attend several meetings, listen to people and look for someone whose recovery seems stable and whose manner feels respectful. You can ask how they approach sponsorship before making a decision. Sponsorship arrangements can also change if the relationship is not helpful.

## Approach the Twelve Steps gradually

The Twelve Steps are AA’s suggested programme of recovery. They involve acknowledging that alcohol has become unmanageable, accepting help, examining behaviour, making appropriate amends and supporting others. Several Steps use spiritual language, including references to God or a “Higher Power”.

AA does not require membership of a religion. Members interpret the spiritual parts in different ways, and some understand a Higher Power as the AA group, the recovery process or something beyond their individual will. If this language is difficult for you, ask other members how they approached it without abandoning your own beliefs.

You are not expected to complete all Twelve Steps immediately. Early recovery may begin with attending meetings, staying away from the first drink and asking for help. A sponsor can later guide you through the Steps at a manageable pace.

## Build a simple recovery plan around meetings

Meetings can provide structure, but the hours between them matter too. Make your immediate plan realistic rather than trying to repair every part of life at once.

Useful early actions may include:

1. **Choose your next meeting before leaving the current one.** Put the time and location in your calendar.
2. **Save a few support numbers.** Include AA contacts alongside professional or personal support.
3. **Reduce exposure to drinking situations.** Change plans temporarily if a place or event is likely to put your sobriety at risk.
4. **Meet basic needs.** Regular meals, hydration, rest and a calmer routine can make difficult feelings easier to manage.
5. **Plan for cravings.** Delay acting, change location, contact someone and remind yourself that an urge does not require a response.
6. **Keep the goal small.** Concentrate on not drinking today rather than solving the rest of your life.

## Consider attending several meetings

AA members sometimes suggest frequent meetings in early recovery. This is not a rule, and the right level of attendance depends on your circumstances, health, responsibilities and wider treatment plan.

Trying different groups can help you find people you relate to and a format that works for you. Some people prefer small discussion meetings; others feel more comfortable listening in a larger group or attending online.

You may eventually choose a **home group**. This simply means a meeting you attend regularly and regard as your main AA group. A home group can provide continuity, familiar faces and opportunities to help with simple tasks such as preparing the room or making refreshments. In AA, these tasks are often called **service**.

## Use other recovery support when needed

AA can offer valuable peer connection, but it does not provide every form of help. Depending on your needs, recovery may also involve:

- A GP or specialist alcohol service
- Medically supported withdrawal or prescribed treatment
- Counselling or psychological therapy
- Residential or community rehabilitation
- Support for mental health, housing, relationships or finances
- Alternative mutual-aid programmes or secular recovery groups

Seeking additional help is not a failure to “do AA properly”. A strong recovery plan can include several forms of support.

## Conclusion

You can start recovery with Alcoholics Anonymous by finding a suitable meeting, attending with an open mind and speaking to one person afterwards. You do not need to understand the entire programme or make promises about the future.

Prioritise medical safety, take recovery one day at a time and try more than one meeting if necessary. Whether AA becomes your main source of support or one part of a broader plan, reaching out today can be a meaningful beginning.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I have to call myself an alcoholic at an AA meeting?

No. You may hear members introduce themselves that way, but a newcomer can simply give a first name or say they are there to listen. You can decide for yourself how to describe your relationship with alcohol.

### Can I attend AA if I drank today?

You can still ask AA for help and attend many meetings, although disruptive behaviour may be addressed by the group. If you are intoxicated, medically unwell or at risk, seek appropriate immediate help and arrange safe transport rather than driving.

### Do I need to believe in God to join AA?

No religious membership or fixed belief is required. AA’s Steps contain spiritual language, but members understand it in varied ways. You can attend, listen and explore the ideas at your own pace.

### What if I do not like my first meeting?

Try another group or format. Meetings differ in size, atmosphere and style, and online meetings may feel different from in-person groups. You can also explore professional treatment and other mutual-aid approaches if AA does not feel right for you.
