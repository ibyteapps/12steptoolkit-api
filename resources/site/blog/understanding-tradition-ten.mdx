Tradition Ten helps Alcoholics Anonymous stay focused on its primary purpose rather than becoming divided by political, religious, social or other public disputes. Its short form states: “Alcoholics Anonymous has no opinion on outside issues; hence the A.A. name ought never be drawn into public controversy.”

In practical terms, this means AA groups and service bodies do not use the Alcoholics Anonymous name to endorse candidates, oppose public policies, support commercial causes or take sides in debates unrelated to AA’s purpose. Individual members remain free to hold and express their own views, but they do not speak for AA as a whole.

For newcomers, Tradition Ten can sound like a rule against discussing difficult subjects. It is not. Members may share personal experiences involving politics, religion, healthcare, family life or social conditions when those experiences relate to alcoholism and recovery. The important distinction is between sharing personal experience and turning an AA meeting into a platform for an outside cause.

## What is AA Tradition Ten?

The Twelve Traditions are principles intended to support the unity and continued functioning of Alcoholics Anonymous groups. While the Twelve Steps focus mainly on personal recovery, the Traditions focus on how AA groups relate to their members, other groups and the wider public.

If you are unfamiliar with the fellowship, this guide to [what Alcoholics Anonymous is and how AA works](/blog/what-is-alcoholics-anonymous-and-how-does-it-work) explains meetings, membership, sponsorship and the Twelve Steps.

Tradition Ten protects AA’s focus. People arrive at meetings with many different backgrounds and beliefs. They may strongly disagree about elections, religion, public health, criminal justice, economics or social movements. If AA took an official position on these subjects, some people seeking help could feel that they had to accept that position before they could belong.

By avoiding opinions on outside issues, AA makes room for people who might agree on very little except their desire to stop drinking and help others recover.

## What counts as an “outside issue”?

An outside issue is generally a subject that does not directly concern AA’s purpose or the functioning of an AA group or service body. Common examples include:

- Political parties, candidates and elections
- Religious doctrines or disputes
- Government policies unrelated to AA operations
- Commercial products and businesses
- Public campaigns and social movements
- Debates about particular treatment providers or recovery organizations

Context matters. A topic is not automatically forbidden merely because it involves politics, medicine or religion. A member might describe how a political argument triggered resentment, how religious experiences affected their recovery, or how professional treatment formed part of their journey. That is personal sharing.

The difficulty arises when someone asks the group to adopt a collective position, endorse a cause or declare that one viewpoint represents Alcoholics Anonymous.

## Tradition Ten does not silence individual members

AA members do not give up their rights, identities or opinions when they enter recovery. They can vote, campaign, join religious communities, support charitable causes and participate in public debate as private individuals.

Tradition Ten asks members to distinguish personal speech from statements made on behalf of AA. For example, an individual can support a proposed law. It would be different to announce that “Alcoholics Anonymous supports this law” without authority to speak for the fellowship.

Even when mentioning personal AA membership, members should consider whether their words could reasonably be mistaken for an official AA position. Public anonymity is also relevant, particularly in press, broadcast media and online publishing. [Tradition Eleven’s guidance on attraction, anonymity and public relations](/blog/understanding-tradition-eleven) explores this subject in more detail.

Tradition Ten is therefore not a demand for personal neutrality. It is a safeguard against attaching the AA name to a public controversy.

## How Tradition Ten works in an AA meeting

AA meetings often include people whose lives have been affected by difficult public and personal events. Compassionate application of Tradition Ten does not require a meeting chair to shut down every reference to an outside subject.

Instead, the meeting can remain centered on alcoholism, sobriety and the AA program. A member might say, “I became consumed by political arguments, and that affected my serenity.” That keeps the focus on personal experience. A lengthy speech intended to persuade everyone to support a party or campaign would move away from the meeting’s recovery purpose.

If sharing becomes divisive, a chairperson might gently remind the room to speak from personal experience and relate contributions to recovery. The aim is not humiliation or censorship. It is to preserve a meeting where people with different beliefs can seek help together.

Meeting formats and practices vary, and AA groups are generally guided by their group conscience—the collective process through which members discuss and decide group matters. Tradition Ten offers a principle rather than a script for controlling conversation.

## What Tradition Ten does not mean

Misunderstanding Tradition Ten can lead to unnecessary silence. It does not mean that an AA group must ignore matters affecting its own meetings.

### It does not prevent attention to safety

A group can discuss harassment, threatening behavior, accessibility, privacy and other concerns that directly affect whether people can participate safely. Addressing conduct within a meeting is not the same as endorsing a wider political campaign.

The Traditions should not be used to discourage someone from contacting appropriate help after violence, abuse or criminal behavior. AA membership does not place anyone beyond the law, and an AA group is not a substitute for emergency services, qualified healthcare, safeguarding support or legal advice.

### It does not make AA a medical authority

Alcoholics Anonymous is a peer fellowship, not a medical provider. AA can explain its own recovery program, but Tradition Ten helps prevent the fellowship from presenting official opinions on competing medical treatments or broader healthcare controversies.

Individual members may describe their experiences with counseling, medication, rehabilitation or other support, provided they make clear that they are speaking personally. Decisions about medical care belong between an individual and appropriately qualified professionals.

### It does not require emotional detachment

Members can care deeply about events beyond AA. The tradition simply encourages them not to recruit the fellowship into those causes. A person can be committed both to AA service and to outside community work while keeping the two roles clear.

## Applying Tradition Ten to social media

Online communication can blur the line between personal identity and AA representation. A personal post can quickly be shared beyond its intended audience, while an AA-named group or meeting page may appear official even when it is locally managed.

Before posting about a controversial subject, it may help to ask:

- Am I speaking only for myself, or could this look like an AA statement?
- Am I using an AA meeting name, logo or service role to add authority to my opinion?
- Is this page intended to communicate meeting information or promote an outside cause?
- Could this post make newcomers believe they must share a particular belief to attend?
- Have I considered both personal anonymity and the anonymity of other members?

A private individual saying, “This is my personal view,” is different from an AA meeting account endorsing a candidate, business or campaign. Disclaimers can help clarify personal speech, although they do not remove the need for thoughtful judgment.

## Why avoiding public controversy supports unity

Tradition Ten is based on practical concerns about unity rather than an assumption that outside issues are unimportant. Public controversies can divide organizations, shift attention away from their main purpose and create barriers for people who need help.

AA includes people from many cultures, faiths, professions and political traditions. A meeting can offer common ground when it concentrates on recovery from alcohol problems. Members do not need to agree about society as a whole in order to listen respectfully and support one another’s sobriety.

This connects closely with [Tradition Twelve and its principle of placing principles before personalities](/blog/understanding-tradition-twelve). Together, these traditions encourage humility, restraint and a focus on shared purpose rather than personal influence.

## Practical ways to use Tradition Ten

Tradition Ten can guide both group decisions and individual conduct. Practical approaches include:

1. **Speak from personal experience.** Use “I” statements rather than claiming that AA agrees with your conclusions.
2. **Connect sharing to recovery.** If an outside subject is relevant, explain how it affected your drinking, thinking, relationships or sobriety.
3. **Avoid campaigning during meetings.** Do not use meeting time, contact lists or AA communication channels to recruit support for unrelated causes.
4. **Separate service roles from personal advocacy.** Make it clear when you are acting privately rather than representing a group or AA service body.
5. **Use group conscience.** If a meeting is uncertain about a public statement or online post, pause and discuss it rather than acting impulsively.
6. **Respond gently to disagreement.** A calm reminder about the meeting’s purpose is usually more helpful than publicly shaming someone.
7. **Protect newcomer inclusion.** Ask whether a reasonable person might conclude that certain political, religious or social beliefs are conditions of belonging.

These practices are not about avoiding responsibility. They help AA remain available to people who may urgently need recovery support, whatever their other views may be.

## Tradition Ten and Step Ten are different

Tradition Ten is sometimes confused with Step Ten because they share the same number. They serve different purposes.

Step Ten concerns personal inventory, recognizing mistakes and making prompt corrections in daily life. Tradition Ten concerns AA’s collective neutrality on outside issues and the use of the AA name in public controversy.

Both encourage self-restraint and responsibility, but one primarily guides individual recovery while the other protects fellowship unity.

## Conclusion

AA Tradition Ten keeps Alcoholics Anonymous focused on helping people recover from alcohol problems. It does not require members to abandon personal beliefs or avoid every difficult subject. Instead, it asks AA groups and members not to present private opinions as the position of Alcoholics Anonymous.

Applied with common sense and compassion, the tradition creates room for people with very different worldviews to meet around one shared purpose: supporting recovery and sobriety.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does Tradition Ten mean politics can never be mentioned in an AA meeting?

No. A member may mention politics as part of an honest account of their experience, especially when it relates to resentment, fear, relationships or sobriety. The meeting should not become a campaign event or adopt a political position in AA’s name.

### Can an AA group address discrimination, harassment or unsafe conduct?

Yes. A group can address behavior that affects participation, safety and the functioning of its meeting. Tradition Ten should not be used to dismiss reports of harassment, threats or abuse. Groups can establish appropriate meeting practices, while serious or urgent concerns may require help from local authorities or qualified services.

### Does Tradition Ten apply to individual AA members outside meetings?

Members remain free to express personal views and participate in outside organizations. The main concern is whether someone uses the AA name or a service position in a way that implies Alcoholics Anonymous officially supports their opinion.

### Who decides whether something is an outside issue?

There is not always a simple universal answer. An AA group can consider the matter through an informed group conscience, asking whether it directly concerns the group’s recovery purpose or operations and whether taking a position could draw the AA name into public controversy.
