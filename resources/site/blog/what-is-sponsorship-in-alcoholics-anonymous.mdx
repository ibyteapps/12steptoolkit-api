Sponsorship in Alcoholics Anonymous is an informal, one-to-one recovery relationship in which a more experienced AA member helps another member understand and work through the Twelve Steps. A sponsor shares personal experience, offers practical encouragement and provides someone to contact when recovery feels difficult.

A sponsor is not a therapist, doctor, life coach or authority figure. They do not control the person they sponsor, make decisions for them or guarantee sobriety. Healthy sponsorship is based on shared experience, honesty, choice and appropriate boundaries.

Sponsorship is common in AA, but it is not a condition of attending meetings or identifying as a member. Practices also vary between groups, communities and countries. Some people find a sponsor quickly, while others take time to meet someone they trust.

## What does “sponsor” mean in AA?

In AA, a sponsor is usually a person who has experience applying the Twelve Steps in their own life and is willing to share that experience with someone else. The person receiving this guidance is sometimes called a “sponsee.”

The relationship is voluntary. Sponsors are not appointed by AA as an organization, and there is no universal certification process. In most cases, one member simply asks another member to sponsor them.

Although sponsors may have substantial experience in sobriety, they remain peers. Their guidance comes from what has helped them—not from professional authority or the power to issue rules.

AA members often describe sponsorship as one person in recovery helping another. That simple idea can include regular conversations, Step work, discussion of AA principles and support through the ordinary challenges of sober living.

## What does an AA sponsor do?

The central role of a sponsor is to guide another member through the Twelve Steps. These are AA’s suggested principles for recovery, involving honest self-examination, making appropriate amends, developing spiritual or reflective practices and helping others.

A sponsor may:

- Explain AA language and customs to a newcomer
- Share how they approached each of the Twelve Steps
- Suggest reading, writing or reflection connected with Step work
- Listen when the sponsee feels afraid, resentful, tempted to drink or uncertain
- Encourage meeting attendance and connection with other people in recovery
- Help the sponsee notice patterns without shaming or diagnosing them
- Share practical experience of navigating daily life without alcohol
- Encourage honesty, responsibility and service to others

A sponsor might ask thoughtful questions rather than provide direct answers. For example, instead of deciding whether someone should leave a job or relationship, a healthy sponsor may help them consider their motives, talk with appropriate professionals and avoid making an impulsive decision during an emotional moment.

## Why is sponsorship important in recovery?

Early sobriety can feel confusing and isolating. Meetings provide connection with a group, while sponsorship offers a more personal relationship with someone who understands AA and has faced similar challenges.

A sponsor may help make the Twelve Steps feel practical rather than abstract. Books and meeting discussions can introduce the principles, but a sponsor can explain how they applied those principles to real situations.

Sponsorship can also support accountability. Knowing that someone may ask how a difficult conversation went—or whether a piece of Step work was completed—can make it easier to follow through. This should be supportive accountability, not monitoring or control.

Another benefit is continuity. People may attend several AA meetings, each with different members and formats. A regular sponsor can become a consistent point of contact as circumstances change.

However, sponsorship is only one form of support. Recovery may also involve healthcare, counseling, medication, peer-support programs, family support, faith communities or secular recovery groups. AA does not need to be a person’s only recovery resource.

## What sponsorship does not include

Understanding the limits of sponsorship is as important as understanding its benefits.

An AA sponsor is not automatically qualified to provide medical, psychiatric, legal, financial or relationship advice. Even if a sponsor works in one of these fields, sponsorship itself is a peer relationship rather than a professional service.

A sponsor should not:

- Diagnose alcohol use disorder or any mental health condition
- Tell someone to begin, stop or change medication
- Give instructions for alcohol withdrawal or detoxification
- Demand money, gifts, work or personal favors
- Control relationships, employment, housing or healthcare decisions
- Insist on access to passwords, finances or private communications
- Use fear, humiliation, threats or romantic pressure
- Claim that disagreement will inevitably lead to drinking
- Require secrecy about conduct that feels unsafe or inappropriate

Abruptly stopping alcohol can be medically dangerous for some people who have become physically dependent. A sponsor cannot assess or manage that risk. Anyone concerned about withdrawal should seek guidance from a qualified healthcare professional or local alcohol and drug service. Severe symptoms or an immediate medical crisis require local emergency help.

Confidentiality is generally valued in sponsorship, but a sponsor is not legally or professionally bound in the same way as a licensed clinician. Before sharing highly sensitive information, it is reasonable to ask how the person understands privacy and its limits.

## How does AA sponsorship usually work?

There is no single schedule that everyone must follow. Some people speak with a sponsor daily during early sobriety, while others arrange weekly conversations and make additional contact when needed. Communication may happen in person, by phone or through online messaging or video calls.

The sponsor may suggest working through the Steps in sequence, using AA literature and written exercises. The exact approach can differ. Some sponsors are structured and set regular tasks; others use a more conversational style.

It helps to discuss expectations early. Useful questions include:

- How often are you usually available to talk?
- How do you guide someone through the Steps?
- What should I do if I cannot reach you during a difficult moment?
- What boundaries are important to you?
- How do you handle private information?
- Are you comfortable with me also using therapy or other recovery support?

No sponsor can be available at all times. A wider support network—such as meeting contacts, trusted friends and professional care—protects recovery from depending entirely on one person.

## How do you find an AA sponsor?

A practical starting point is to attend several meetings and listen for people whose recovery appears steady, honest and respectful. Look beyond confidence or popularity. Someone may be a good speaker but not have the availability or temperament to sponsor effectively.

Potentially helpful qualities include:

- Personal experience of working the Twelve Steps
- Stable recovery habits
- Willingness to listen as well as speak
- Respect for personal choice and professional healthcare
- Dependability without unrealistic promises
- Clear emotional, financial and sexual boundaries
- An approach to spirituality that you can discuss honestly
- Humility about the limits of their experience

You do not need to know someone extremely well before asking. You can request a short conversation after an AA meeting and say, “I’m looking for help working the Steps. Would you be willing to sponsor me?” It is also acceptable to ask whether the person would consider temporary sponsorship while you get to know each other.

A person may say no because they are busy, already sponsor several people or do not feel they are the right fit. This is not necessarily a rejection of you. Ask another member or tell the meeting secretary or trusted group member that you are looking for sponsorship.

## What does a healthy sponsor-sponsee relationship look like?

Healthy sponsorship supports independence rather than dependence. Over time, the sponsee should become better able to apply recovery principles, make considered choices and support others—not increasingly afraid to act without permission.

Both people should be able to communicate openly. A sponsee can ask why a suggestion is being made, explain when something feels uncomfortable and decline advice. A sponsor can set limits on availability and acknowledge when an issue requires professional help.

Disagreement does not automatically mean the relationship has failed. People have different personalities and experiences. The important question is whether differences can be handled with honesty and mutual respect.

The relationship should also remain focused on recovery. Romantic or sexual pressure, financial entanglement, manipulation and demands for obedience are warning signs. If you feel unsafe, step back, speak with trusted AA members or an appropriate professional, and seek another sponsor.

## Can you change your AA sponsor?

Yes. Sponsorship is voluntary, and you may change sponsors if the relationship is not helpful, safe or compatible with your recovery needs. You do not need permission from AA, your group or your current sponsor.

People change sponsors for many reasons: availability changes, communication styles differ, boundaries are crossed or a different approach to the Steps becomes necessary. When it feels safe, a brief and respectful conversation is often enough. You might say, “Thank you for the support you have given me. I’ve decided to work with someone else.”

Try to maintain other recovery connections while making the transition. Guidance on [changing an AA sponsor respectfully and safely](/blog/can-you-change-your-aa-sponsor) can help you plan the next step.

## Sponsorship is support, not perfection

Neither sponsors nor sponsees need to be perfect. A sponsor can make mistakes, have difficult days and say, “I don’t know.” In fact, the willingness to acknowledge limits can be a sign of healthy sponsorship.

The relationship works best when both people bring honesty and realistic expectations. The sponsor offers experience and guidance; the sponsee remains responsible for their choices, safety and wider recovery plan.

## Conclusion

Sponsorship in Alcoholics Anonymous is a voluntary peer relationship designed primarily to help one member guide another through the Twelve Steps. A good sponsor can offer experience, encouragement and a steady point of contact, but cannot replace medical care, therapy or personal decision-making.

If you are new to AA, take time to listen, ask questions and look for someone whose recovery and conduct you respect. The right relationship should help you feel supported, honest and increasingly capable of participating in your own recovery.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do you have to have a sponsor to be in AA?

No. Sponsorship is widely encouraged and many members find it valuable, but you can attend AA meetings without a sponsor. You may take time to meet people and decide whether sponsorship is right for you.

### How long should an AA sponsor be sober?

AA does not impose one universal sobriety requirement for sponsors. More important considerations include whether the person has worked through the Twelve Steps, has stable recovery experience, respects boundaries and has enough availability to help. Local customs may vary.

### Can an AA sponsor be a friend?

Friendship may develop, but the sponsorship relationship should retain a clear recovery purpose. A sponsor should be able to offer honest guidance without controlling you, and both people should maintain appropriate boundaries and connections outside the relationship.

### Can a sponsor tell you what to do?

A sponsor can make suggestions based on personal recovery experience, particularly about the Twelve Steps. They are not entitled to control your healthcare, relationships, finances or other life decisions. You remain responsible for your choices and may seek professional advice whenever needed.
