An AA sponsor is an Alcoholics Anonymous member who shares their experience of staying sober and helps another member work through the Twelve Steps. A sponsor may listen, offer practical suggestions, explain unfamiliar AA language and encourage honest action. They are not a therapist, doctor, life coach or authority figure, and they do not run another person’s recovery.

Sponsorship is usually a voluntary, one-to-one relationship between equals. The sponsor has generally worked—or is actively working—the Twelve Steps and passes on what helped them. Exact approaches vary because sponsors are not licensed, certified or employed by Alcoholics Anonymous.

Understanding what a sponsor can and cannot do can help you enter the relationship with realistic expectations and healthy boundaries.

## What is an AA sponsor?

A sponsor is a fellow AA member who provides personal guidance based on lived experience. In AA language, the person being sponsored is sometimes called a **sponsee**.

The relationship is intended to support recovery from alcohol problems, especially through the Twelve Steps. It is different from simply having a friend at meetings. A sponsor will often ask direct questions, suggest specific Step work and encourage you to apply recovery principles in everyday life.

You do not need to understand the entire AA program before asking for a sponsor. Many people begin with a temporary sponsor while they attend meetings and get to know other members. If you are unsure how to approach someone, this guide explains [how to ask someone to sponsor you in AA](/blog/how-to-ask-someone-to-sponsor-you).

## What does an AA sponsor actually do?

Although every sponsorship relationship is different, several responsibilities are common.

### Explains the Twelve Steps

The main purpose of sponsorship is usually to guide a person through the Twelve Steps using the sponsor’s own experience and understanding of AA literature.

A sponsor may help you explore what each Step means, suggest writing or reflection, and discuss questions that arise. They are not expected to have perfect answers. Their role is to show how they personally applied the Steps and help you find an honest way to apply them yourself.

For example, a sponsor might help a newcomer understand the language of powerlessness in Step One, approach the personal inventory in Step Four, or prepare carefully for amends in Steps Eight and Nine. For a broader overview, see [the Twelve Steps of Alcoholics Anonymous explained simply](/blog/the-twelve-steps-of-alcoholics-anonymous-explained).

### Shares experience rather than issuing orders

A healthy sponsor commonly says, in effect, “This is what worked for me,” rather than insisting that there is only one correct choice.

They may make firm suggestions, particularly when they recognize patterns that contributed to their own drinking. These might include attending meetings, contacting other sober people, avoiding high-risk situations and beginning Step work instead of waiting to feel completely ready.

However, you remain responsible for your decisions. A sponsor should not control where you live, whom you date, what job you take, how you spend money or whether you follow professional medical advice.

### Provides regular recovery contact

Many sponsors and sponsees speak regularly by phone, text, video call or in person. In early sobriety, contact may be frequent because everyday situations can feel unfamiliar and cravings or difficult emotions may arise quickly.

Conversations might include:

- How you are feeling and whether you have considered drinking
- Meetings you have attended
- Progress with the Steps
- Resentment, fear, dishonesty or isolation
- Upcoming situations that could challenge sobriety
- Practical recovery actions for the day

There is no universal contact schedule. Some sponsors ask newcomers to call daily for a period, while others arrange several conversations each week. Availability, time zones, family responsibilities and accessibility all matter. Agreeing on a realistic rhythm can prevent confusion. Our guide to [how often you should speak to your AA sponsor](/blog/how-often-should-you-speak-to-your-sponsor) explores this in more detail.

### Listens without pretending to be a professional

A sponsor can be an understanding listener because they have personal experience of alcohol problems and recovery. They may recognize rationalizations, avoidance or fear that people outside recovery do not immediately understand.

But lived experience is not the same as professional training. Sponsors should not diagnose alcohol use disorder, mental health conditions or physical illness. They should not advise you to begin, stop or change medication, or tell you to ignore a healthcare professional.

If you may be physically dependent on alcohol, seek qualified medical guidance before abruptly stopping. Alcohol withdrawal can be dangerous for some people. Severe symptoms, a medical crisis, thoughts of self-harm or immediate danger require local emergency services or qualified professional help—not only a sponsor call.

### Encourages connection with the wider fellowship

A sponsor is one part of a recovery network, not the entire network. They may introduce you to other members, suggest AA meetings, encourage service and help you become less isolated.

This matters because sponsors sometimes become unavailable. They work, sleep, travel, become ill and have personal responsibilities. Building several healthy connections means your sobriety does not depend on one person answering every call.

### Offers honest feedback and accountability

A sponsor may notice when your words and actions do not match. They might challenge excuses, ask whether you have been fully honest or remind you of an action you agreed to take.

Healthy accountability is direct but respectful. It is not humiliation, punishment or forced obedience. The purpose is to help you recognize patterns and act in line with your recovery values.

### Models recovery in ordinary life

Sponsors do not need perfect lives. In fact, sponsorship can demonstrate how recovery principles are practiced amid work pressures, relationships, disappointment and mistakes.

A sponsor may model admitting when they are wrong, making amends, asking for help and continuing to attend meetings. The value lies in practical example rather than presenting themselves as an expert who has solved every problem.

## What an AA sponsor does not do

Clear limits protect both people. An AA sponsor is generally not meant to be:

- A doctor, therapist, detoxification provider or crisis service
- A financial adviser, lawyer or religious authority
- A romantic or sexual partner within the sponsorship relationship
- A source of loans, housing or employment
- Available every hour of every day
- Responsible for keeping another person sober
- Entitled to control personal decisions
- Automatically qualified to handle trauma, abuse or psychiatric symptoms

A sponsor can help you identify when outside support is appropriate. Depending on your needs, this could include a physician, licensed therapist, addiction specialist, local alcohol and drug service, peer-support group or another recovery program. AA can be valuable, but it is not the only route to recovery and can be combined with evidence-based healthcare.

## What does working with a sponsor look like?

A first conversation often covers expectations. You might ask how the sponsor works the Steps, how often they prefer contact and what to do if they do not answer. You can also discuss whether meetings will be in person or remote.

After that, the sponsor may suggest reading relevant AA material and discussing it together. Some use written questions or structured assignments; others rely more on conversation. Practices differ across countries, groups and individuals.

You may hear suggestions such as:

1. Contact the sponsor at an agreed frequency.
2. Attend AA meetings consistently.
3. Begin working the Steps rather than postponing them indefinitely.
4. Call before drinking when possible, not only afterward.
5. Develop additional sober contacts.
6. Be honest when something is unclear or uncomfortable.

These suggestions should support your participation, not remove your agency.

## Privacy and sensitive information

AA places a strong value on anonymity, and many sponsors treat conversations as private. However, a sponsor is not automatically bound by the same legal confidentiality rules as a licensed therapist, attorney or healthcare professional.

Before sharing highly sensitive information, ask how the person understands privacy and whether there are circumstances in which they would seek outside help. Laws and professional duties vary by location, particularly if a sponsor separately works in a regulated profession.

Step Five involves admitting the nature of past wrongs to a Higher Power, oneself and another person. That listener is often a sponsor, but it does not always have to be. Consider safety, trust and appropriate professional support, especially where trauma, abuse, crime or danger is involved.

## Signs of a healthy sponsorship relationship

Healthy sponsorship usually includes:

- Respectful honesty in both directions
- Clear expectations about calls and availability
- Focus on sobriety and the Twelve Steps
- Freedom to ask questions or disagree
- Encouragement to build a wider support network
- Appropriate personal, financial and sexual boundaries
- Willingness to involve professionals when needed
- Recognition that neither person is perfect

Pay attention if a sponsor demands secrecy, money, obedience or social isolation; pressures you into romance or sexual contact; discourages medical care; threatens you; or uses shame to control you. Such behavior is not made acceptable by being described as recovery guidance.

You may set limits, speak with trusted AA members or choose another sponsor. Changing sponsors does not mean you have failed, and you do not need to remain in a relationship that feels unsafe or consistently unhelpful.

## How to get the most from sponsorship

Sponsorship works best as active cooperation rather than passive advice-seeking. Try to call when agreed, complete Step work honestly and explain when you do not understand a suggestion.

It also helps to discuss problems early. If you feel ignored, controlled or mismatched, describe the specific issue rather than allowing resentment to build. Sometimes a straightforward conversation resolves it. Sometimes a different sponsor is the healthier choice.

Most importantly, do not wait for perfect confidence. Sponsorship is a relationship in which trust often grows through small, consistent actions.

## Conclusion

An AA sponsor is a sober guide through the Twelve Steps, a source of lived experience and an honest recovery contact. A good sponsor supports action without taking control, respects boundaries and recognizes when professional care is needed.

Sponsorship can become an important part of sobriety, but it works best alongside meetings, a wider support network and any appropriate medical or psychological care.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does an AA sponsor have to be sober for a certain number of years?

AA has no universally enforced certification or minimum sobriety period for sponsors. Many people look for someone with stable sobriety who has worked the Twelve Steps and has a sponsor of their own. Experience matters, but sobriety length alone does not guarantee a healthy fit.

### Can an AA sponsor tell me what to do?

A sponsor can make clear suggestions based on experience, but they should not control your life. You remain responsible for medical, financial, legal, family and relationship decisions. Relevant professionals should handle matters outside the sponsor’s experience.

### Is everything I tell my sponsor confidential?

Many sponsors take privacy seriously, but they are not automatically protected or legally bound in the way some professionals are. Discuss privacy expectations before sharing sensitive information, and use qualified professional support when legal, medical, trauma or safety issues are involved.

### Can I have more than one sponsor?

Most people have one primary AA sponsor to avoid conflicting Step guidance, but they may also speak with temporary sponsors, experienced members and other trusted people. Customs vary. The important point is to build broad support without creating confusion about who is guiding your Step work.
