Step Ten of Alcoholics Anonymous is about maintaining recovery through regular self-examination. Its wording is: “Continued to take personal inventory and when we were wrong promptly admitted it.” In practical terms, this means noticing your behaviour, taking responsibility for mistakes and dealing with problems before they grow.

The Step is not an invitation to criticise yourself constantly or accept blame for everything. It is a daily practice of honest awareness. By recognising resentment, fear, dishonesty or harmful behaviour early, you can choose a healthier response rather than allowing old patterns to build towards drinking.

## What does Step Ten mean?

Step Ten brings the principles of the earlier Steps into everyday life. Instead of treating personal inventory as a one-off exercise, it encourages continuing reflection.

In AA, an “inventory” is an honest review of your thoughts, motives and actions. The term comes from the idea of checking stock: noticing what is useful, what is causing difficulty and what may need to change. It is not simply a list of personal faults. A balanced inventory can also recognise progress, kindness, courage and actions that support sobriety.

The word “continued” matters. Recovery is not completed after working the first nine Steps. Life keeps changing, relationships remain imperfect and difficult emotions still arise. Step Ten offers a way to respond without expecting perfection.

If the overall structure is unfamiliar, [the Twelve Steps of Alcoholics Anonymous explained simply](/blog/the-twelve-steps-of-alcoholics-anonymous-explained) provides a newcomer-friendly overview.

## How Step Ten builds on the earlier Steps

Steps Four to Nine involve examining personal patterns, discussing them honestly, becoming willing to change and making amends where appropriate. Step Ten carries that work into daily living.

You might think of the earlier Steps as a thorough clearing-out and Step Ten as regular maintenance. Rather than waiting until resentment, secrecy or conflict becomes overwhelming, you address it closer to the time.

This does not mean repeating every earlier Step in full each day. It means using the same principles—honesty, willingness, responsibility and humility—as situations occur.

Step Ten also leads naturally into Step Eleven, which focuses on prayer, meditation and seeking guidance. One Step reviews how you are living; the other helps create space for direction and a calmer response. Our guide to [Step Eleven and daily guidance](/blog/understanding-step-eleven-of-alcoholics-anonymous) explains that connection in more detail.

## What is a personal inventory?

A personal inventory is a deliberate pause to look at what is happening within you and how you are behaving towards other people.

Useful questions may include:

- Am I holding on to resentment?
- Have I been frightened, defensive or dishonest?
- Was I unkind, impatient or inconsiderate?
- Am I trying to control another person?
- Is there something I am avoiding?
- Do I owe anyone an acknowledgement or apology?
- What did I handle well today?
- What support do I need now?

The purpose is not to label every uncomfortable emotion as wrong. Anger, fear, grief and frustration are normal human experiences. The useful question is often what you do with the feeling. You can experience anger without behaving cruelly, and you can feel afraid while still asking for help.

A healthy inventory considers both impact and responsibility. You are accountable for your conduct, but you are not automatically responsible for another person’s mood, choices or reaction.

## Three practical ways to practise Step Ten

AA members do not all practise Step Ten in exactly the same way. Some use brief checks during the day, while others set aside time in the evening. Many combine both approaches.

### 1. Use a spot-check inventory

A spot-check inventory is a short pause taken when something feels unsettled. You might notice tension after an argument, a strong urge to prove yourself right or resentment replaying in your mind.

Try pausing and asking:

1. What happened?
2. What am I feeling?
3. What part of this belongs to me?
4. Is there anything I need to admit or correct?
5. What would a sober, constructive next action be?

This pause can interrupt an automatic response. You may decide to take a few breaths, leave a heated conversation temporarily, telephone someone supportive or return later with a calmer attitude.

### 2. Review the day in the evening

A short evening review can help you recognise patterns. It does not need to become a lengthy written exercise.

You could consider:

- Where was I honest and helpful today?
- Where did fear or resentment influence me?
- Did I cause harm that needs attention?
- Is there something I should discuss with a sponsor or trusted person?
- What can I learn without punishing myself?

Writing a few private notes may help, but it is not essential. If journalling turns into repetitive self-criticism, a shorter verbal review with someone trustworthy may be more useful.

### 3. Ask for another perspective

In AA, a sponsor is another member who offers guidance based on their own experience of working the Steps. A sponsor is not a therapist, doctor or authority over your life, but they may help you distinguish genuine responsibility from excessive guilt.

You might also speak with a trusted recovery peer, counsellor, faith leader or another suitable source of support. The important point is not to remain isolated with thoughts that are becoming confused or overwhelming.

## What does “promptly admitted it” involve?

A prompt admission means acknowledging your part without unnecessary delay. It does not require an impulsive conversation while emotions are still running high.

A useful admission is usually clear and specific. For example:

> “I interrupted you and spoke disrespectfully. That was wrong. I am sorry.”

This is more accountable than saying, “I am sorry you were upset,” which focuses on the other person’s reaction rather than your behaviour.

Try to avoid adding excuses or demanding immediate forgiveness. The other person may need time, and their response is outside your control. Your task is to be honest and change what you can.

Promptness should still include judgement and safety. If contact could put you or someone else at risk, breach an important boundary or create further harm, seek guidance before acting. An admission is not permission to re-enter an unsafe relationship.

## Is admitting a mistake the same as making amends?

They are related but not always identical. An admission acknowledges what you did. An amends is an effort to repair harm or change your behaviour.

For a small everyday mistake, a sincere apology and corrected action may be enough. Other situations may require more thought. Repair could involve returning something, correcting false information, meeting a neglected responsibility or demonstrating consistent change over time.

Step Ten does not mean repeatedly apologising for the same behaviour while making no effort to change it. The deeper aim is to live differently.

For serious, complicated or legally sensitive matters, it can be wise to obtain appropriate guidance before taking action. AA experience can offer recovery support, but it does not replace professional legal, safeguarding or clinical advice.

## Common misunderstandings about Step Ten

### “I must be wrong whenever there is conflict”

Step Ten asks you to examine your part, not to take responsibility for everybody involved. You may need to admit an unkind response while still maintaining a reasonable boundary or recognising that another person behaved badly.

### “I should monitor every thought”

The Step is not about achieving perfect thoughts or constant emotional control. It is intended to increase awareness and improve action. Excessive self-monitoring can become exhausting and unhelpful.

### “An inventory should focus only on faults”

Recognising progress supports realistic recovery. Notice when you pause instead of reacting, tell the truth, attend a meeting, ask for help or handle a difficult situation without drinking.

### “Promptly means immediately”

Sometimes an immediate acknowledgement is appropriate. At other times, you may need to calm down, speak with a sponsor or consider the safest approach. Prompt action means not avoiding responsibility indefinitely.

## How Step Ten can support sobriety

Unresolved resentment, shame, fear and isolation can make recovery harder. Step Ten gives these experiences somewhere to go: they can be noticed, discussed and addressed.

The practice can also make daily life feel more manageable. Instead of trying to solve your entire past or guarantee lifelong sobriety, you deal with the next honest action. This fits closely with the AA emphasis on staying sober today, explored in [practical tools for living one day at a time](/blog/how-to-stay-sober-one-day-at-a-time).

Step Ten is not a guarantee against drinking, and AA is not the only path to recovery. Some people combine meetings and Step work with support from their GP, alcohol services, counselling, medication or other peer-support communities. The right combination varies from person to person.

If you are drinking heavily or think you may be physically dependent, seek medical guidance before suddenly stopping. Alcohol withdrawal can be dangerous for some people, and AA meetings are not a substitute for medical assessment or supervised care.

## A simple Step Ten routine

A manageable routine might look like this:

- **Pause:** Notice agitation, resentment, fear or defensiveness.
- **Name:** Identify what you are feeling without judging yourself.
- **Review:** Ask what part of the situation is yours.
- **Admit:** Acknowledge a mistake clearly when appropriate.
- **Repair:** Take a reasonable action to put matters right.
- **Share:** Speak with a sponsor or trusted supporter if you are unsure.
- **Continue:** Learn from the event and return to the present day.

Consistency is more useful than perfection. Missing an evening review does not mean you have failed. You can begin again at the next available moment.

## Conclusion

Step Ten of Alcoholics Anonymous turns recovery principles into a continuing daily practice. Through regular inventory and prompt responsibility, small problems can be addressed before they become larger ones.

The Step is not about living under constant self-criticism. It is about becoming more honest, accountable and free to make different choices—one situation and one day at a time.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need to write a Step Ten inventory every night?

No single method suits everyone. Some AA members write a short evening review, some reflect quietly and others speak with a sponsor. A simple and repeatable practice is often more sustainable than an elaborate routine.

### What if I cannot identify my part in a conflict?

Give yourself time and discuss the situation with someone trustworthy. Another perspective may reveal something you missed, but it may also confirm that you are taking on responsibility that is not yours.

### Should I apologise every time I feel guilty?

Not automatically. Guilt can point towards a genuine mistake, but it can also be excessive or misplaced. Consider what you actually did, whether contact is safe and what action would genuinely repair rather than increase harm.

### Can I practise Step Ten if I am not religious?

Yes. Step Ten itself centres on inventory and responsibility. AA includes spiritual language, but members understand and approach spirituality in different ways. You can begin with practical honesty, reflection and willingness to change.
