Alcoholics Anonymous Tradition Five says: “Each group has but one primary purpose—to carry its message to the alcoholic who still suffers.” In simple terms, every AA group exists mainly to help people with alcohol problems find the possibility of recovery through the AA program.

This primary purpose gives a group direction. Meetings may differ in size, format, culture and style, but Tradition Five asks them to keep one central question in view: **Does what we are doing help carry the AA message to someone who may need it?**

Tradition Five does not mean that every group must operate identically, solve every problem or persuade people to join AA. It means that AA groups stay most useful when they concentrate on what they are equipped to offer: one person with experience of recovery sharing hope and practical experience with another.

## What is AA Tradition Five?

Tradition Five is one of the Twelve Traditions of Alcoholics Anonymous. The Twelve Steps guide individual recovery, while the Twelve Traditions help AA groups remain united, focused and available to future members.

The Tradition contains several important ideas:

- Each AA group has a defined purpose.
- That purpose is to carry the AA message.
- The message is intended especially for people who still suffer from alcohol problems.
- Other worthwhile activities should not displace this central work.

For newcomers, “carrying the message” generally means sharing personal experience of alcoholism, sobriety and working the Twelve Steps. It may happen during an AA meeting, in a conversation after the meeting, through sponsorship or as part of organized service.

If AA language is unfamiliar, this guide to [what Alcoholics Anonymous is and how AA works](/blog/what-is-alcoholics-anonymous-and-how-does-it-work) explains meetings, sponsorship and the Twelve Steps in more detail.

## What is the “primary purpose” of an AA group?

A primary purpose is the main reason something exists. An AA group may provide friendship, routine, practical support and a sense of belonging, but these benefits grow around its central purpose of carrying the recovery message.

Keeping this purpose clear helps a group decide how to use its meeting time, voluntary contributions and service positions. For example, members might ask:

- Is the meeting welcoming and accessible to newcomers?
- Can members share honestly about recovery from alcohol problems?
- Are unrelated debates taking attention away from AA’s purpose?
- Do meeting formats help people learn about the Twelve Steps?
- Is there a clear way for newcomers to ask questions or find support?

Tradition Five is therefore both a statement of purpose and a practical decision-making tool.

It does not require a perfect meeting. Groups are made up of people, and people sometimes disagree, lose focus or make mistakes. The Tradition gives members a shared point to return to when deciding what matters most.

## What is “the AA message”?

AA members may describe the message in different words. At its heart, it is that recovery from alcoholism is possible and that the Twelve Steps, fellowship and mutual support have helped many members live without alcohol.

Carrying that message is not the same as giving orders, making promises or presenting one member’s experience as a universal rule. An AA member can describe:

- What drinking was like for them
- What led them to seek help
- How they became willing to change
- Which AA practices supported their sobriety
- What life in recovery is like today

This is sometimes summarized as sharing “experience, strength and hope.” Rather than claiming to know exactly what another person must do, a member explains what helped them.

The message is also carried through behavior. A newcomer may notice whether members listen, respect confidentiality, avoid pressure and make space for questions. A calm welcome or a conversation after an AA meeting can communicate hope as strongly as a formal talk.

## Who is “the alcoholic who still suffers”?

In AA language, this means a person experiencing the effects of alcoholism, whether or not they currently identify as an alcoholic or have stopped drinking.

It may include:

- Someone attending their first meeting
- A person who is unsure whether they belong
- A returning member after drinking again
- A sober member struggling emotionally or losing connection with recovery
- Someone trying to find help in a remote or underserved community

Tradition Five encourages groups not to reserve care only for confident, established members. People who are anxious, uncertain or returning after an absence may especially need a welcoming response.

AA does not clinically diagnose alcohol use disorder, and members are not substitutes for healthcare professionals. AA is also not the only path to recovery. People may benefit from medical care, therapy, medication, rehabilitation, peer-support alternatives, culturally specific services or a combination of approaches.

Anyone concerned about withdrawal should seek professional medical guidance. Abruptly stopping alcohol can be dangerous for some people who have become physically dependent. Severe symptoms or an immediate safety concern require local emergency services.

## How Tradition Five shapes an AA meeting

A group can put Tradition Five into practice without becoming rigid. The aim is purposeful service, not perfection.

### Creating a welcoming first contact

Newcomers may arrive feeling frightened, ashamed or skeptical. Members can help by introducing themselves, explaining the meeting format and making clear that listening is acceptable. Nobody needs to understand AA language immediately.

Groups can also explain the difference between open and closed meetings. Open meetings generally welcome interested visitors, while closed meetings are intended for people who have a desire to stop drinking. Local practices and meeting descriptions may vary, so newcomers can ask the group or meeting contact if unsure.

### Keeping sharing relevant

People bring their whole lives into recovery, so AA sharing naturally includes relationships, work, grief, health and emotions. Tradition Five does not mean members can discuss only alcohol.

The helpful question is whether the sharing connects those experiences to alcoholism, sobriety, the Twelve Steps or spiritual and emotional growth in recovery. A meeting can acknowledge real-life difficulties without turning primarily into a forum for unrelated causes or disputes.

### Making service support the message

Setting up chairs, moderating an online meeting, greeting people, maintaining a contact list and arranging speakers may appear to be small tasks. Together, they make it possible for the message to reach someone who needs it.

This understanding can make routine service feel meaningful. The purpose is not status or control; it is keeping the meeting available and useful.

### Remaining focused around outside organizations

AA groups may interact with treatment facilities, healthcare settings, community centers or correctional institutions. Tradition Five helps clarify that cooperation does not require endorsement, ownership or involvement in another organization’s affairs.

This focus works alongside [Tradition Six and its guidance on outside interests](/blog/understanding-tradition-six). A group can provide information about AA or make meetings available while preserving its own purpose and independence.

## What Tradition Five does not mean

Misunderstandings can make the Tradition seem narrower or harsher than intended. Tradition Five does not mean:

- **AA members cannot have other interests.** Individual members can support causes, follow political or religious beliefs and participate in community life in their own names.
- **Every conversation must mention the Steps.** Fellowship and ordinary human connection can help people feel safe enough to return.
- **Groups should ignore serious safety concerns.** Meeting safety, appropriate boundaries and access to emergency or professional help still matter.
- **Members should pressure others into AA.** The message is offered through example and shared experience, not coercion.
- **AA must replace treatment.** Mutual-help fellowship and professional care can complement each other.
- **Only newcomers still suffer.** Long-term sobriety does not make someone immune to isolation, fear or difficulty.

The Tradition concerns the purpose of the AA group. It is not a rule that prevents individual members from being full participants in family, professional and community life.

## Tradition Five and Step Twelve

Tradition Five is closely connected to Step Twelve, which includes carrying the AA message to people experiencing alcoholism. The difference is one of emphasis: Step Twelve guides the individual member, while Tradition Five guides the group.

An individual may carry the message by sponsoring, speaking at a meeting, answering a newcomer’s call or sharing honestly. A group creates the structure in which that work can continue over time.

Neither requires a member to rescue another person or guarantee an outcome. Carrying the message means making recovery available; it does not mean controlling whether someone accepts it. Our guide to [Step Twelve, service and daily practice](/blog/understanding-step-twelve-of-alcoholics-anonymous) explores this distinction further.

## Practical ways groups can apply Tradition Five

Groups can periodically review how well their meeting serves its primary purpose. This may happen during a group conscience meeting—a discussion in which members consider group matters and seek an informed collective decision.

Useful questions include:

1. Can newcomers easily understand what happens at this meeting?
2. Are meeting details accurate and available through appropriate local channels?
3. Do members know how to welcome someone attending for the first time?
4. Is the format focused enough to communicate recovery experience?
5. Are a few personalities dominating the meeting?
6. Can people with disabilities or other access needs participate where reasonably possible?
7. Are online and hybrid meetings protecting privacy and reducing unnecessary barriers?
8. Does the group connect willing newcomers with appropriate sources of support?

Individual members can also practise Tradition Five by listening without judgment, avoiding unsolicited medical advice and speaking from personal experience. When asked for help beyond their competence, they can encourage the person to contact a qualified professional or appropriate local service.

## Why Tradition Five still matters

Groups face changing technology, social expectations and local needs. Meetings now take place in community rooms, healthcare settings, correctional facilities, homes and online spaces across many countries.

The method of communication may change, but Tradition Five offers a stable test: does this help make the AA recovery message available to the person who still suffers?

That focus protects both clarity and inclusion. It reminds established members why the meeting exists and helps ensure that a confused or frightened newcomer is not overlooked.

## Conclusion

AA Tradition Five gives every group a clear primary purpose: carrying its recovery message to people who still suffer from alcoholism. It encourages welcoming meetings, relevant sharing, humble service and thoughtful cooperation without asking AA to become all things to all people.

For members and groups, the Tradition is a simple compass. When choices become complicated, returning to primary purpose can help place recovery, hope and the newcomer at the center.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Tradition Five only about helping newcomers?

No. Newcomers are an important focus, but a person who still suffers may also be a returning member or someone who has been sober for years and is struggling. The message remains available at every stage of recovery.

### Can an AA meeting discuss topics other than alcohol?

Yes. Recovery affects relationships, emotions, work, spirituality and daily life. Sharing generally remains consistent with Tradition Five when members connect these subjects to alcoholism, sobriety or practising the AA program.

### Does carrying the message mean trying to convince people to join AA?

No. Members normally carry the message by sharing their own experience and making AA available. The person hearing it remains free to decide whether AA is right for them. Pressure, promises and attempts to control someone are not necessary.

### How can one member practise Tradition Five?

A member can welcome newcomers, listen respectfully, share personal recovery experience, take on meeting service and remain available within healthy boundaries. They can offer the AA message while recognizing when medical, psychological or emergency support is needed.
