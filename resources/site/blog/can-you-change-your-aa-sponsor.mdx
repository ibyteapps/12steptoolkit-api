Yes, you can change your AA sponsor. Sponsorship is a voluntary relationship, and you do not need to remain with someone who is unavailable, incompatible with your needs, disrespectful of your boundaries or no longer helping you work toward sobriety.

Changing sponsors does not mean you have failed, betrayed anyone or rejected Alcoholics Anonymous. Sometimes the relationship simply is not a good fit. At other times, your recovery needs change. The goal is to make the transition honestly, respectfully and without leaving yourself isolated.

## What does an AA sponsor do?

In Alcoholics Anonymous, a sponsor is usually another AA member who has experience with sobriety and the Twelve Steps. They share how they have applied the AA program, help a sponsee—the person being sponsored—work through the Steps, and provide practical encouragement.

A sponsor is not a therapist, physician, emergency service, financial adviser or personal authority. They should not diagnose conditions, manage medication, control relationships or make every decision for a sponsee. Although a sponsor may offer suggestions based on personal experience, you remain responsible for your choices.

Sponsorship is an informal AA practice, so relationships vary. Some sponsors encourage daily contact in early recovery, while others arrange a weekly conversation. Some work closely from AA literature; others take a less structured approach. Local customs may differ, but mutual respect and appropriate boundaries matter everywhere.

If you are still deciding whom to approach, this guide to [asking someone to sponsor you in AA](/blog/how-to-ask-someone-to-sponsor-you) explains what to look for and what you might say.

## Is it acceptable to change your AA sponsor?

Yes. There is generally no formal AA procedure requiring permission to change sponsors. You can end one sponsorship relationship and ask another member to sponsor you.

It is understandable to feel guilty or nervous, particularly if your sponsor helped you through a difficult period. Gratitude, however, does not create a lifelong obligation. A healthy sponsor recognizes that the relationship exists to support recovery rather than meet the sponsor’s emotional needs.

You might also worry about gossip, awkwardness at an AA meeting or appearing unwilling to take guidance. Before deciding, it can help to ask yourself whether you are avoiding a difficult but useful part of Step work—or responding to a genuine problem in the relationship. An honest conversation with another trusted AA member can provide perspective.

## Reasons you might change sponsors

There does not have to be a dramatic incident. Common reasons include:

- Your sponsor is regularly unavailable or does not return calls.
- You want more consistent guidance through the Twelve Steps.
- Your approaches to spirituality, meetings or Step work are not compatible.
- Communication repeatedly leaves you confused, shamed or dismissed.
- Your sponsor is trying to control your healthcare, finances or relationships.
- Personal, romantic, sexual or financial boundaries have become blurred.
- Your sponsor has returned to drinking and cannot currently offer stable support.
- One of you has moved, changed schedules or developed other commitments.
- You have grown in different directions and another sponsor may be a better fit.
- The relationship feels dependent rather than supportive of your responsibility and growth.

Differences do not always require a change. A sponsor will not necessarily agree with you, and constructive challenge can be valuable. The question is whether the relationship supports honesty, safety and progress—or consistently creates fear, control, confusion or stagnation.

## Warning signs that call for stronger action

Some situations are more serious than a simple mismatch. Consider stepping away promptly if a sponsor:

- Threatens, intimidates, humiliates or sexually harasses you.
- Pressures you into romantic or sexual contact.
- Requests money, access to accounts or inappropriate favors.
- Demands secrecy about harmful behavior.
- Tells you to stop prescribed medication or ignore professional care.
- Tries to isolate you from safe friends, family, meetings or clinicians.
- Uses sponsorship to exercise excessive control over your daily life.

You do not owe an in-person meeting or extended explanation when you feel unsafe. You can end contact by phone or message, block further communication if necessary, and seek support from trusted people. Depending on the circumstances, you may also choose to inform an appropriate meeting or group contact. If there is an immediate threat, contact local emergency services or another suitable local safeguarding resource.

AA peer support does not replace healthcare. Decisions about medication, mental health treatment or medically supervised alcohol withdrawal belong with qualified professionals. Abruptly stopping alcohol can be dangerous for some people who have become physically dependent, so seek medical guidance if withdrawal may be a concern.

## How to change your AA sponsor respectfully

### 1. Clarify what is not working

Write down your main concerns. Try to distinguish between a temporary difficulty and a continuing pattern.

You might ask:

- Am I receiving enough guidance to work the Steps?
- Can I speak honestly without fearing punishment or humiliation?
- Are my boundaries respected?
- Do I follow through on reasonable commitments as a sponsee?
- Have I clearly discussed what I need?
- Is this relationship helping me take responsibility for my recovery?

Knowing why you want a change can help you choose differently next time.

### 2. Get perspective from a trusted person

Talk confidentially with an experienced AA member, meeting contact, therapist or other trusted recovery supporter. Avoid recruiting people to take sides. The purpose is to gain perspective and protect your recovery, not to build a case against your sponsor.

If your difficulty involves ordinary communication, you may decide to discuss it with your current sponsor first. If it involves coercion, harassment or safety, you do not need to attempt reconciliation.

### 3. Arrange continuing support

Try not to create an unnecessary gap, especially in early sobriety. Attend meetings, speak with supportive members and consider asking someone to act as a temporary sponsor while you decide.

A temporary sponsor can offer short-term guidance without requiring either person to make an immediate long-term commitment. You can also strengthen your broader support network so that no single person carries your entire recovery.

For additional ideas, see these [practical ways to be a good AA sponsee](/blog/how-to-be-a-good-aa-sponsee), including honest communication, consistent contact and healthy boundaries.

### 4. Tell your sponsor directly when appropriate

If the relationship is safe, a brief and respectful conversation is usually enough. You do not need to debate every disagreement or secure approval.

You could say:

> “Thank you for the time and support you have given me. I have decided to work with a different sponsor because I need a different approach at this stage of my recovery.”

Or:

> “I appreciate your help. I do not think this sponsorship arrangement is the right fit for me, so I am going to ask someone else.”

If asked for feedback, you can be honest without becoming hostile. For example: “I need more regular contact and a more structured way of working through the Steps.”

### 5. Ask a new sponsor clear questions

Before beginning again, discuss expectations. Useful questions include:

- How do you usually guide someone through the Twelve Steps?
- How often do you expect contact?
- What should I do when you are unavailable?
- How do you understand confidentiality and boundaries?
- Are you comfortable with my use of professional healthcare or other recovery support?
- What do you expect from a sponsee?

No sponsor will be perfect. Look for someone whose sobriety, conduct and approach you respect—not someone who claims to have every answer.

## What if your sponsor reacts badly?

Your sponsor may feel surprised or disappointed. They are allowed to have feelings, but you do not have to reverse your decision to manage those feelings.

If they become argumentative, repeat your decision calmly: “I understand that you see it differently, but I have decided to make a change.” End the conversation if it turns insulting, manipulative or threatening.

Try not to criticize your former sponsor around the AA community. You can state simply that you needed a different approach. However, discretion does not mean concealing behavior that could put you or another person at risk. Serious boundary violations can be discussed with appropriate trusted people.

## How to protect your recovery during the transition

Changing sponsors can bring relief, grief, uncertainty or all three. Keep your recovery routine steady while those feelings settle.

Practical steps include:

- Continue attending AA meetings or other peer-support groups.
- Save the contact details of several safe recovery supporters.
- Maintain regular meals, sleep and daily structure where possible.
- Keep working on manageable recovery actions rather than rushing major Step decisions alone.
- Be honest if resentment, cravings or thoughts of drinking increase.
- Use professional counseling or healthcare alongside AA when needed.

AA can be valuable, but it is not the only recovery pathway. Some people combine it with therapy, medication prescribed by a clinician, rehabilitation, secular mutual-aid groups, faith communities or other support. If you are new to the fellowship, [how to start recovery with Alcoholics Anonymous](/blog/how-to-start-your-recovery-with-alcoholics-anonymous) explains meetings, sponsorship and early support.

If you feel close to drinking, contact safe support immediately, go to a meeting if one is available, and move away from situations where alcohol is easily accessible. If you are in immediate danger or unable to stay safe, contact local emergency services or an appropriate crisis service in your country.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need my AA sponsor’s permission to change sponsors?

No. Sponsorship is voluntary, and you can choose to work with someone else. If it is safe to do so, communicating your decision directly and respectfully can provide closure, but you do not need permission.

### Can I have more than one AA sponsor?

Practices vary. Some people have one primary sponsor and also speak with several experienced AA members. Having multiple sources of support can be healthy, but conflicting Step guidance may become confusing. Be transparent about the arrangement and decide who is helping you work through the Steps.

### Should I change sponsors if we disagree?

Not necessarily. Respectful disagreement can encourage growth. Consider whether your sponsor listens, explains their experience without trying to control you, and respects your final decisions. Repeated humiliation, coercion or serious boundary violations are different from an honest disagreement.

### Can I return to a previous sponsor later?

Possibly, if both people agree and the relationship is safe. Circumstances and recovery needs can change. Before restarting, discuss what did not work previously and establish clearer expectations rather than assuming the old arrangement will now be different.

## Conclusion

You can change your AA sponsor whenever the relationship is no longer safe, workable or supportive of your recovery. When possible, make the change with honesty, gratitude and clear boundaries. Most importantly, stay connected to meetings, trusted people and any professional support you need while finding a sponsor who can help you continue building sobriety.
