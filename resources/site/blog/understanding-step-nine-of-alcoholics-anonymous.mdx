Step Nine of Alcoholics Anonymous is about taking responsible action to address harm caused in the past. It involves making direct amends to people wherever possible, unless doing so could cause further injury to them or someone else.

For newcomers, the idea can feel frightening. You may imagine difficult conversations, rejection or having to reveal every detail of your past. Step Nine is not intended to be a rushed round of apologies or a way to ease guilt at another person’s expense. It is a careful process of accepting responsibility, repairing what can reasonably be repaired and changing how you behave.

Most people work this Step with guidance from an AA sponsor—an experienced member who supports another person through the Twelve Steps. Professional advice may also be appropriate when an amend involves legal, financial, safeguarding or workplace concerns.

## What is Step Nine of Alcoholics Anonymous?

Step Nine says:

> “Made direct amends to such people wherever possible, except when to do so would injure them or others.”

An amend is more than saying sorry. An apology expresses regret; an amend aims to put something right. Depending on the situation, that might mean repaying money, replacing property, correcting misinformation, acknowledging dishonesty or consistently behaving differently.

The words “wherever possible” and “except when to do so would injure them or others” are essential. AA does not ask people to pursue contact regardless of the consequences. A direct conversation may not be safe, welcome or appropriate. The welfare and privacy of other people matter.

Step Nine follows the preparation of Step Eight, in which a person identifies those they have harmed and becomes willing to make amends. If you are unfamiliar with the wider programme, [the Twelve Steps of Alcoholics Anonymous explained simply](/blog/the-twelve-steps-of-alcoholics-anonymous-explained) provides a newcomer-friendly overview.

## What is the purpose of making amends?

Step Nine encourages accountability without demanding endless self-punishment. Its purpose is not to prove that you are a bad person or to secure forgiveness. It is to face the effects of past behaviour honestly and do what you reasonably can to repair them.

Making amends may help you:

- take responsibility without excuses;
- reduce the need to hide from past actions;
- repair practical or financial damage where possible;
- rebuild trust through consistent behaviour;
- learn how your actions affected other people;
- develop honesty, humility and consideration.

Amends do not guarantee reconciliation. The other person may be angry, cautious or unwilling to respond. They may accept your amend without wanting further contact. Step Nine asks you to take responsibility for your side, not to control their reaction.

## The difference between an apology and an amend

A sincere apology can be part of an amend, but the two are not identical.

An apology might say, “I am sorry that I failed to keep my promise.” An amend adds accountability: “I was unreliable and caused you difficulty. I would like to repair what I can, and I am changing how I handle commitments.”

A useful amend usually avoids:

- blaming alcohol as though it removes all responsibility;
- explaining so much that the explanation becomes an excuse;
- asking the other person to reassure or forgive you;
- listing their mistakes in return;
- disclosing unnecessary details that could cause fresh harm;
- making promises you cannot realistically keep.

The strongest evidence of change often comes afterwards. Arriving when you say you will, respecting boundaries, meeting financial commitments and speaking honestly can matter more than a polished speech.

## Why Step Nine should not be rushed

The urge to “clear the slate” quickly is understandable, but speed can make an amend self-serving or unsafe. Early recovery can also involve strong emotions and uncertain judgement.

Before contacting anyone, many AA members discuss each proposed amend with their sponsor. A sponsor can help examine your motive, consider possible consequences and decide whether direct contact is appropriate. They can also help you distinguish genuine restitution from an attempt to restart a relationship or obtain relief from guilt.

Consider questions such as:

- Who could be affected by this amend?
- Is direct contact safe and appropriate?
- Am I expecting forgiveness, renewed contact or reassurance?
- What specific harm am I taking responsibility for?
- Is there a practical way to repair some of the damage?
- Could revealing details violate another person’s privacy?
- Do I need professional advice before acting?

If you are new to the fellowship and do not yet have support, [a newcomer’s guide to starting recovery with Alcoholics Anonymous](/blog/how-to-start-your-recovery-with-alcoholics-anonymous) explains meetings, asking for help and the role of a sponsor.

## How to make a direct amend

There is no single script that suits every situation. A simple structure can nevertheless help you prepare.

### 1. Name your behaviour clearly

Focus on what you did rather than making broad statements about being a terrible person. For example, you might acknowledge that you borrowed money and did not repay it, repeatedly broke commitments or spoke dishonestly about someone.

Clear language shows responsibility. It also keeps the conversation focused on the harm rather than on your shame.

### 2. Avoid conditions and excuses

An amend loses its meaning when followed by “but you did…” or “if you had not…”. Other people may have behaved imperfectly, but Step Nine is about your conduct.

You can provide necessary context without using it to escape responsibility. Alcohol problems may help explain what was happening, but they do not erase the impact on others.

### 3. Ask what can reasonably be put right

Where appropriate, ask whether there is something you can do to repair the harm. Do not promise an outcome you cannot deliver.

Financial restitution, for example, may require a realistic repayment arrangement rather than an impressive promise that soon collapses. If debts, contracts, criminal matters or employment issues are involved, seek appropriate legal, financial or professional guidance before making contact or commitments.

### 4. Listen without defending yourself

The person may describe effects you did not recognise. Listening can be uncomfortable, but it is part of accepting responsibility. You do not have to agree with every interpretation, nor should you remain in an abusive or unsafe interaction.

If the conversation becomes threatening or harmful, it is reasonable to end it and seek guidance.

### 5. Respect the response

The other person has the right to decline contact, take time or set boundaries. An amend is not permission to enter someone’s life again.

Do not repeatedly contact a person who has asked you to stop. Respecting that limit may itself be part of your amends.

## When direct amends may cause further harm

The safety clause in Step Nine requires careful judgement. Direct contact may be inappropriate where it could expose someone to danger, reopen trauma, breach confidentiality, destabilise a family or disregard a clear no-contact boundary.

Extra caution is needed in situations involving:

- abuse, coercion, stalking or threats;
- children or vulnerable adults;
- restraining orders or formal no-contact arrangements;
- infidelity or secrets affecting third parties;
- criminal, legal or serious financial consequences;
- a person who has explicitly refused contact;
- someone who has died or cannot be located.

A sponsor can offer recovery experience, but they are not a substitute for a solicitor, clinician, safeguarding professional or qualified financial adviser. Where risk is present, obtain suitable professional guidance. Never break the law or ignore a person’s boundaries in the name of completing a Step.

Choosing not to make direct contact does not necessarily mean avoiding responsibility. It may mean finding a safer, indirect way to repair harm.

## Indirect amends and living amends

An indirect amend addresses harm without approaching the person directly. This might include repaying a debt through an agreed channel, correcting a false statement, returning property or contributing positively without seeking recognition.

A living amend means consistently changing the behaviour that caused harm. If you were absent from family life, that might involve becoming reliably present while respecting relatives’ boundaries. If dishonesty was the problem, it means practising honesty even when it is inconvenient.

When a person has died, you might write an unsent letter, discuss the situation with your sponsor, repair related harm to others or live according to values that honour what you have learnt. Symbolic gestures should not replace practical restitution where practical repair remains possible.

## Handling fear, shame and expectations

Anxiety before an amend is normal. Preparation can help, but waiting to feel completely fearless may result in indefinite delay. The aim is not perfect confidence; it is responsible action taken with support.

Try to separate remorse from shame. Remorse says, “I did something harmful and want to change.” Shame says, “I am beyond repair.” Step Nine is built around the possibility that people can acknowledge harm and begin living differently.

Keep expectations realistic. Some conversations bring relief or improved relationships. Others are painful or inconclusive. A person may not forgive you, and you may need to accept that consequence without returning to alcohol or retaliating.

On emotionally difficult days, focus on immediate support and simple routines. These [practical tools for staying sober one day at a time](/blog/how-to-stay-sober-one-day-at-a-time) may help you protect your recovery before and after a challenging amend.

If you are physically dependent on alcohol, stopping abruptly can be dangerous. Seek medical guidance rather than trying to manage withdrawal alone. AA can provide peer support, but it does not replace medical care, counselling or other professional treatment. AA is also not the only route to recovery.

## After making an amend

Give yourself time to process what happened. Speak to your sponsor or another trusted source of support, attend a meeting if that helps, and avoid obsessively interpreting every response.

Then follow through. Pay what you agreed, honour boundaries and correct old patterns promptly when they reappear. Later Steps support this continuing practice: Step Ten, for example, involves reviewing your conduct and admitting mistakes without allowing them to accumulate.

Step Nine is therefore not a one-off performance. It marks a shift from regret alone towards responsible, sustained change.

## Conclusion

Step Nine of Alcoholics Anonymous is a careful process of making amends without creating further harm. It asks for honesty, courage and humility, but it does not require reckless disclosure or forced reconciliation.

Work slowly, seek guidance and respect the rights of everyone involved. A sincere amend cannot rewrite the past, but practical restitution and changed behaviour can help build a more trustworthy life in recovery.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I have to make every Step Nine amend face to face?

No. Direct contact may be appropriate in many cases, but not where it could cause injury or violate boundaries. Depending on the circumstances, an indirect or living amend may be safer. Discuss the situation with a sponsor and seek professional advice where necessary.

### What if the person does not forgive me?

Forgiveness is their choice. Your responsibility is to acknowledge your conduct, make reasonable restitution and respect their response. Do not pressure someone to comfort you, resume a relationship or accept your amend.

### Should I contact someone who has asked me to leave them alone?

No. A clear boundary or legal restriction must be respected. Repeated contact may create further harm. Seek guidance about whether a safe indirect or living amend is possible.

### Can I make Step Nine amends without a sponsor?

It is possible, but experienced guidance is strongly encouraged because guilt or urgency can make consequences difficult to assess. A sponsor can offer an AA perspective. Legal, clinical, safeguarding or financial questions should be taken to an appropriately qualified professional.
