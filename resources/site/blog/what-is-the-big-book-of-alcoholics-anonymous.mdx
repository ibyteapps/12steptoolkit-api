The **Big Book of Alcoholics Anonymous** is the main foundational text used in Alcoholics Anonymous (AA). Its formal title is *Alcoholics Anonymous: The Story of How More Than One Hundred Men Have Recovered from Alcoholism*, although it is almost always called the “Big Book.”

First published in 1939, it describes AA’s approach to recovery from alcohol problems, presents the Twelve Steps and includes personal accounts from people who found sobriety. AA members often read it individually, discuss it with a sponsor and use it in meetings focused on the book.

You do not need to understand or agree with every page before attending an AA meeting. The Big Book is not a test, a medical manual or a set of membership rules. It is a recovery text written in the language of its time, and many newcomers understand it gradually through discussion and practical experience.

## Why is it called the Big Book?

The nickname came from the size of the first edition. It was printed on unusually thick paper and appeared physically large, which helped give rise to the informal name “Big Book.” The nickname remained even though later editions were produced in different formats.

The formal title, *Alcoholics Anonymous*, also gave the fellowship its name. Today, “AA” can refer both to the worldwide fellowship and to the recovery program described in its literature.

The book is published by Alcoholics Anonymous World Services. Editions and translations may vary by country, particularly in the selection of personal stories, but the central explanation of the AA program remains broadly consistent.

## What is in the AA Big Book?

The Big Book has several distinct sections. Knowing what each part is for can make the book feel much less intimidating.

### The opening material

The front of the book includes prefaces and forewords describing the development and growth of Alcoholics Anonymous. Different editions contain different forewords, offering historical context about how AA expanded from a small number of members into an international fellowship.

This section is useful for understanding AA history, but newcomers do not have to begin there.

### “The Doctor’s Opinion”

This section presents a physician’s historical perspective on alcoholism. It helped early AA members describe why determination alone did not seem sufficient to control their drinking.

Some of its medical terminology reflects the 1930s and should not be treated as current clinical guidance. Modern healthcare professionals may use terms such as **alcohol use disorder**, which can cover a range of symptoms and severity. The Big Book cannot diagnose a person or replace an assessment from a qualified professional.

### The first 164 pages

AA members often refer to the “first 164 pages.” This is the core portion of the book explaining the recovery program and how the Twelve Steps may be applied.

Its chapters discuss subjects such as:

- The experience of repeatedly losing control over drinking
- Why willpower may not have been enough
- Hope and belief in the possibility of recovery
- The Twelve Steps
- Personal inventory and admitting harms
- Making amends where doing so will not cause further harm
- Prayer, meditation and continued self-examination
- Helping other people with alcohol problems
- Relationships, work and everyday life in sobriety

The first 164 pages do not offer a quick self-help formula. They present a sequence of spiritual and practical actions intended to be taken with honesty, support and care. For an accessible overview, see [the Twelve Steps of Alcoholics Anonymous explained simply](/blog/the-twelve-steps-of-alcoholics-anonymous-explained).

### Personal recovery stories

Later sections contain first-person accounts from AA members. These stories describe different patterns of drinking, consequences and paths into recovery.

The details vary widely. Some writers experienced severe financial, medical or relationship losses. Others recognised their problem before losing everything. The purpose is not to suggest that every reader must have the same history. Instead, readers can look for similarities in thoughts, feelings and drinking patterns.

Story selections have changed across editions, and editions published for different regions may contain locally relevant experiences. This reflects the fact that alcohol problems and recovery affect people across cultures and backgrounds.

### Appendices

The appendices cover several topics connected with AA, including its spiritual approach, traditions and service structure. They also provide historical and explanatory material that may become more meaningful after someone has attended meetings or begun working the Steps.

## What does the Big Book mean by recovery?

In the Big Book, recovery generally means more than simply not drinking. Abstinence from alcohol is central, but the text also describes changes in attitudes, relationships, behavior and daily living.

Its proposed approach includes:

- Accepting the reality of a drinking problem
- Becoming willing to receive help
- Examining resentments, fears and harmful behavior
- Sharing honestly with a trusted person
- Repairing harm where it is safe and appropriate
- Developing ongoing spiritual or reflective practices
- Supporting others
- Practicing honesty, responsibility and service in daily life

AA members do not all describe these changes in exactly the same way. Some use explicitly religious language, while others understand spirituality as connection, shared values, nature, conscience or support beyond individual willpower. The book contains frequent references to God, but many AA groups welcome people who are religious, agnostic or atheist.

## How is the Big Book used in AA?

There is no single required reading routine for every AA member. Common approaches include the following.

### Reading with a sponsor

In AA, a **sponsor** is another member who shares their experience of working the Twelve Steps. A sponsor is not a therapist, doctor or authority figure. They may read the Big Book with a newer member, explain unfamiliar AA language and discuss how passages relate to practical Step work.

Sponsorship is voluntary, and arrangements differ. A healthy sponsor should respect privacy, personal boundaries and the need for professional help when an issue is outside their experience.

### Big Book meetings

A Big Book meeting is an AA meeting centered on reading and discussing the text. The group may study a few paragraphs, a chapter or a personal story. Members then share their experience and understanding.

Meeting formats vary. Some ask for volunteers to read aloud; others have a designated reader. Nobody should be required to read or speak. Newcomers can usually listen quietly if that feels more comfortable.

### Individual reading

Some people read a few pages each morning or evening. Others take notes, underline passages or write questions for a sponsor or meeting discussion.

Slow reading is often more useful than trying to finish quickly. Older expressions can be difficult, and some passages make more sense after hearing how other people have applied them.

If you are opening the book for the first time, this [newcomer’s guide to starting the AA Big Book](/blog/how-to-start-reading-the-aa-big-book) offers a simple reading order and practical note-taking ideas.

## Is the Big Book outdated?

Parts of the Big Book can sound dated because the core text was written in the late 1930s. It often reflects the social assumptions, family roles and masculine language of that era. Certain medical explanations also predate modern understanding of alcohol use disorder.

Readers respond to this in different ways. Some value the historical language and continuity of the original program. Others focus on the underlying principles while supplementing the book with modern healthcare, counseling, inclusive recovery literature or other peer-support approaches.

It is reasonable to question language that feels excluding or difficult. A sponsor or experienced group member may explain how they interpret it, but you remain responsible for your own beliefs, healthcare decisions and personal boundaries.

## Do you have to read the Big Book to join AA?

No. AA membership does not require passing a test, completing the book or accepting every statement in it. AA describes its membership requirement as a desire to stop drinking.

You can attend an open AA meeting simply to learn more. Closed meetings are intended for people who have a desire to stop drinking, while open meetings may also welcome family members, professionals and interested observers. Local meeting listings normally identify the format.

Owning a book is not generally required to attend. Some groups provide books for use during meetings, and official AA service offices may offer print, digital, audio or accessible editions. Availability and cost vary by country, so check an official AA website or local AA service office for your region.

## Is the Big Book the only path to recovery?

No. The Big Book presents the Alcoholics Anonymous approach, but AA is not the only route to recovery. People may also benefit from medical care, counseling, medication prescribed by a qualified clinician, residential or outpatient treatment, secular peer-support groups, faith communities or combinations of these resources.

AA can complement professional care, but meetings and sponsors do not replace doctors, mental health professionals or emergency services.

If you drink heavily or regularly and are considering stopping, seek medical guidance. Abruptly stopping alcohol can cause dangerous withdrawal for some dependent drinkers. Contact a qualified healthcare professional, local alcohol and drug support service, or local emergency services if severe symptoms or immediate danger are present.

## Practical tips for reading the Big Book

Try these newcomer-friendly approaches:

1. **Begin with a short section.** Ten minutes of attentive reading may be more useful than forcing an entire chapter.
2. **Notice similarities rather than differences.** The writers’ lives may differ from yours, but their thoughts or patterns may feel familiar.
3. **Write down unfamiliar terms.** Bring questions to a sponsor, trusted member or Big Book meeting.
4. **Separate principles from period language.** Ask what practical idea a passage is trying to communicate.
5. **Avoid using the book to diagnose yourself or others.** Discuss health concerns with a qualified professional.
6. **Test ideas through safe action.** Recovery literature becomes more useful when paired with support, reflection and responsible daily choices.
7. **Explore other options too.** It is acceptable to combine AA participation with professional treatment or another recovery community.

## Conclusion

The AA Big Book is the foundational text of Alcoholics Anonymous. It explains the Twelve Step approach, describes recovery as a continuing way of living and shares personal stories of sobriety.

Newcomers do not have to understand it immediately or agree with every line. Reading slowly, attending a Big Book meeting and discussing difficult passages with a trusted person can make it more accessible. Most importantly, the book is a resource—not a substitute for medical care, personal judgment or the wider range of support available in recovery.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the real name of the AA Big Book?

Its formal title is *Alcoholics Anonymous: The Story of How More Than One Hundred Men Have Recovered from Alcoholism*. “Big Book” is an informal nickname dating back to the physically large first edition.

### Where should a newcomer start reading the Big Book?

Many newcomers begin with “The Doctor’s Opinion,” “Bill’s Story,” “There Is a Solution,” “More About Alcoholism” and “How It Works.” There is no compulsory order, and reading with a sponsor or discussion group can help clarify unfamiliar language.

### Are the Twelve Steps in the Big Book?

Yes. The Twelve Steps appear in the chapter “How It Works,” and later chapters explain how early AA members applied them. The book also discusses inventory, amends, prayer, meditation and helping others.

### Can I attend AA without owning or reading the Big Book?

Yes. You do not need to own the book, finish it or understand it before attending an AA meeting. Many people first encounter it at a meeting, and some groups make copies available for use during the session.
