Step Seven of Alcoholics Anonymous is about humbly asking a Higher Power to remove our shortcomings. In practical terms, it invites us to stop trying to manage every part of recovery through willpower alone and become willing to live differently.

The Step reads: **“Humbly asked Him to remove our shortcomings.”**

For newcomers, words such as “humbly”, “Him” and “shortcomings” can feel religious, old-fashioned or uncomfortable. Yet Step Seven does not have to mean putting yourself down or waiting passively for instant transformation. It can be understood as an active practice of honesty, openness, humility and changed behaviour.

This Step follows the self-examination of earlier Steps and the willingness developed in Step Six. It helps turn that willingness into a daily way of living.

## What is Step Seven of Alcoholics Anonymous?

Step Seven is the seventh part of the Twelve Step programme used by Alcoholics Anonymous. It focuses on asking for help with the habits, attitudes and reactions that can make sober living more difficult.

These might include resentment, dishonesty, fear, pride, impatience, controlling behaviour, avoidance or self-centredness. AA members often call these patterns “shortcomings” or “character defects”. Those terms refer to unhelpful patterns of behaviour; they do not mean that a person is defective, worthless or beyond help.

If you are unfamiliar with the wider programme, [the Twelve Steps of Alcoholics Anonymous explained simply](/blog/the-twelve-steps-of-alcoholics-anonymous-explained) provides a newcomer-friendly overview.

Step Seven is not a demand to become perfect. It is an invitation to recognise that some familiar ways of coping may no longer serve us, and to seek help in responding differently.

## How Step Seven follows Step Six

Steps Six and Seven are closely connected.

In Step Six, a person becomes ready to let go of their shortcomings. In Step Seven, they ask for help in doing so. The movement is from **readiness** to **request**, followed by practical action.

For example, someone may recognise that resentment has become a repeated problem. Step Six involves becoming willing to release the resentment rather than defending it. Step Seven involves asking for help, pausing when resentment returns and choosing a more constructive response.

This does not mean that difficult emotions immediately disappear. Change often happens gradually. A person may notice the old reaction sooner, act on it less often or put matters right more quickly. That is still meaningful progress.

## What does humility mean in Step Seven?

Humility is central to Step Seven, but it is often misunderstood.

Humility does not mean humiliation, shame or pretending that you have no strengths. It means seeing yourself honestly: neither superior nor inferior to other people. It also means accepting that you may need support and that you do not control every outcome.

In recovery, humility might look like:

- Asking another person for help before a problem escalates
- Admitting that your first reaction may not be the most helpful one
- Listening without immediately defending yourself
- Accepting responsibility without attacking yourself
- Recognising both your strengths and your limitations
- Being teachable, even when feedback is uncomfortable
- Focusing on your own conduct rather than managing everyone else

Healthy humility protects dignity. It says, “I am responsible for my choices, I can learn, and I do not have to do this alone.”

## What does “Him” mean if you are not religious?

The wording of Step Seven uses “Him” because it appears in the original formulation of the Steps. Many people understand this as God, while others use broader ideas of a Higher Power.

Within recovery, a Higher Power may be understood in different ways. Some people think of a traditional deity. Others focus on the collective wisdom of their recovery fellowship, spiritual principles, nature or a source of meaning greater than their individual will.

You do not need to force certainty that you do not have. If spiritual language feels difficult, you might begin with willingness: *I am open to help beyond my usual habits and thinking.*

It can also help to speak with a sponsor or experienced AA member about how they approached the spiritual language. A sponsor is another AA member who informally guides someone through the Steps based on their own experience. They are not a therapist, doctor or authority figure.

Some people use a traditional Step Seven prayer. Others speak in their own words or sit quietly with the intention to change. The essential point is honest willingness rather than finding a perfect formula.

## What are shortcomings in AA recovery?

Shortcomings are patterns that repeatedly cause harm, block connection or threaten emotional and spiritual wellbeing. They are not simply every trait you dislike about yourself.

A useful distinction is between an emotion and the behaviour that follows it. Feeling angry, frightened or hurt is part of being human. A shortcoming may be the repeated response to that feeling, such as lashing out, lying, withdrawing without explanation or trying to control another person.

Examples might include:

- Turning fear into avoidance or dishonesty
- Allowing pride to prevent an apology
- Using resentment to justify unkind behaviour
- Seeking control when feeling uncertain
- Becoming defensive when someone raises a concern
- Making promises to gain approval and then not keeping them

The purpose of noticing these patterns is not to create a new list of reasons for shame. It is to identify where growth is possible.

## How to practise Step Seven

Step Seven may involve prayer or meditation, but it is also expressed through ordinary choices. The following suggestions can help make it practical.

### Discuss the Step with a sponsor

Talking through Step Seven can uncover misunderstandings, particularly the belief that humility means self-criticism. A sponsor may help you identify where genuine willingness is present and where you are still holding tightly to an old behaviour.

### Ask for help in simple language

Your request does not need to sound formal. You might say:

- “Help me respond with patience today.”
- “Help me let go of the need to be right.”
- “Help me be honest about my part in this.”
- “Help me act from courage rather than fear.”

Use language that fits your beliefs. Sincerity matters more than eloquence.

### Pause before reacting

When an old pattern appears, create a short gap between feeling and action. Take a breath, leave the room briefly if appropriate, phone a supportive person or write down what you want to say before saying it.

A pause does not remove the shortcoming by itself. It creates room for a different choice.

### Choose the opposite action

If impatience is the pattern, practise listening. If avoidance is the pattern, make the necessary phone call. If pride blocks an apology, acknowledge your part plainly. If isolation is becoming familiar, contact someone safe.

Small repeated actions are often more useful than dramatic promises.

### Review the day without punishment

At the end of the day, consider:

- Where did an old pattern appear?
- Did I pause or ask for help?
- Was anyone affected by my behaviour?
- What could I try differently tomorrow?
- What progress can I acknowledge?

This kind of review later becomes part of Step Ten, which involves continued personal inventory and prompt admission of mistakes. The guide to [Step Ten and daily inventory](/blog/understanding-step-ten-of-alcoholics-anonymous) explains that ongoing practice in more detail.

## What Step Seven does not mean

Step Seven can be harmful if it is interpreted as a reason to accept mistreatment or ignore genuine needs. It does not mean:

- Blaming yourself for everything that has happened
- Remaining in an unsafe or abusive situation
- Suppressing normal emotions
- Excusing another person’s harmful behaviour
- Expecting every difficulty to disappear immediately
- Replacing professional medical or psychological support
- Waiting passively while continuing the same conduct

Humility includes recognising when specialist help is needed. AA offers peer support for recovery from alcohol problems, but it is not a substitute for healthcare, counselling or emergency assistance. It is also not the only route to recovery; people may use professional treatment, other peer-support groups, medication where clinically appropriate, therapy or a combination of approaches.

If you may be physically dependent on alcohol, seek medical guidance before stopping abruptly, as withdrawal can be dangerous for some people.

## How Step Seven supports later Steps

Step Seven prepares the ground for repairing relationships. As defensiveness, blame or avoidance begin to loosen, it may become easier to look honestly at the effects of past behaviour.

Step Eight asks a person to identify those they have harmed and become willing to make amends. It is not yet the stage of approaching people. The practical guide to [understanding Step Eight](/blog/understanding-step-eight-of-alcoholics-anonymous) explains how to create a thoughtful harm list and prepare safely for later amends.

The humility developed in Step Seven can help someone approach this work without minimising harm or collapsing into shame. Both extremes keep the focus on the self. Humility makes room for responsibility, care and proportion.

## Signs that Step Seven is becoming part of daily life

There is rarely a single moment when every shortcoming disappears. More often, change becomes visible in everyday situations.

You may notice that you:

- Admit mistakes sooner
- Feel less compelled to win every disagreement
- Ask for support before reaching a crisis
- Recover more quickly after an emotional reaction
- Become more curious about other people’s perspectives
- Make fewer excuses for familiar behaviour
- Accept progress without demanding perfection

Old patterns may still return, particularly during stress. Their return does not prove that the Step has failed. It is another opportunity to notice, ask for help and practise a different response.

## Conclusion

Step Seven of Alcoholics Anonymous combines humility with action. It asks us to recognise the limits of willpower, seek help with harmful patterns and practise healthier responses in daily life.

It is not about becoming flawless or treating yourself as defective. It is about becoming more honest, teachable and willing to change. Over time, these small choices can support sobriety, strengthen relationships and prepare the way for the Steps that follow.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need to believe in God to take Step Seven?

You do not need to pretend to hold beliefs that you do not have. AA members understand a Higher Power in varied ways. You can explore the Step through willingness, spiritual principles, the support of a group or another understanding that is meaningful to you.

### How long does Step Seven take?

There is no universal timetable. Some people formally work through it with a sponsor, but continue practising its principles throughout recovery. Shortcomings often change gradually through awareness, support and repeated action.

### Does asking for shortcomings to be removed mean I do nothing else?

No. Step Seven is not passive. Asking for help is usually accompanied by concrete choices, such as pausing before reacting, telling the truth, seeking support, apologising when appropriate and practising a different behaviour.

### What if I keep repeating the same shortcoming?

Repeated patterns are common and do not automatically mean you have failed. Discuss the pattern honestly with a sponsor, trusted recovery contact or appropriate professional. Look for triggers, reduce opportunities to act impulsively and focus on one manageable alternative response at a time.
