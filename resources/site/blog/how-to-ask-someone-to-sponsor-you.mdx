Asking someone to sponsor you in Alcoholics Anonymous can feel intimidating, but it does not need to be complicated. In most cases, you can simply say: **“I’m looking for a sponsor. Would you be willing to sponsor me?”**

You do not need a perfect speech, a certain length of sobriety or a complete understanding of the Twelve Steps before asking. Sponsorship is generally an informal, voluntary relationship in which one AA member helps another understand and work through the AA program based on personal experience.

It is reasonable to talk with someone first, ask how they approach sponsorship and take time to decide whether the relationship feels appropriate. If they say no, it usually reflects their availability or circumstances—not your worth or your chances of recovery.

## What does an AA sponsor do?

An AA sponsor is a member who shares their experience of staying sober and working the Twelve Steps. Sponsors often help newer members:

- Understand AA language and principles
- Begin working through the Twelve Steps
- Use AA tools in everyday life
- Stay connected with meetings and other members
- Talk honestly about challenges in sobriety
- Recognize when professional or emergency help is needed

Sponsorship is not a professional service or a formal AA appointment. A sponsor is not automatically a therapist, doctor, legal adviser, financial adviser or authority over your life. They should not control your healthcare, relationships, employment, finances or personal beliefs.

Practices vary among AA members, groups and countries. Some sponsors use a structured approach with regular meetings and written Step work. Others are more flexible. Neither style is automatically right for everyone, but expectations should be discussed clearly.

If you are new to the fellowship, learning [what Alcoholics Anonymous is and how AA works](/blog/what-is-alcoholics-anonymous-and-how-does-it-work) can make the idea of sponsorship feel less mysterious.

## How to choose someone to ask

You do not need to find a flawless person. Look for someone whose recovery appears steady, honest and grounded in AA principles.

A potential sponsor may be a good fit if they:

- Have experience working the Twelve Steps
- Attend AA meetings consistently
- Seem to apply recovery principles outside meetings
- Listen without shaming or dominating
- Respect privacy and personal boundaries
- Are willing to share what helped them without claiming to know everything
- Can be honest while remaining compassionate
- Have enough time and availability to sponsor you

Many people choose someone with whom they can identify and speak openly. Some AA communities commonly suggest choosing a sponsor whose gender or relationship context reduces the possibility of romantic or sexual complications. This is a custom rather than a universal rule, and it may not fit everyone, including some LGBTQ+ members. The practical goal is to choose a safe, respectful relationship that keeps the focus on recovery.

Try not to choose only on the basis of popularity, personality or an impressive story. Pay attention to how the person treats newcomers, responds to disagreement and behaves over time.

## Listen before you ask

If possible, attend several meetings and listen to different people share. Notice whose description of recovery is practical and relatable. You can introduce yourself after a meeting and start with a brief conversation.

Useful questions include:

- “Do you currently sponsor people?”
- “How do you usually take someone through the Steps?”
- “How often do you expect a sponsee to contact you?”
- “Do you prefer calls, messages or meeting in person?”
- “What do you expect from someone you sponsor?”
- “How do you handle privacy and boundaries?”

You are not interviewing an employee, and the other person is not evaluating you for admission. These questions simply help both of you understand whether the arrangement may work.

If you want more context before beginning, this guide to [the Twelve Steps explained simply](/blog/the-twelve-steps-of-alcoholics-anonymous-explained) describes the process a sponsor may help you explore.

## What to say when asking someone to sponsor you

Choose a reasonably private moment before or after an AA meeting, or contact the person if they have already given you their phone number. Be direct and keep the request simple.

You could say:

> “I appreciate what you share about recovery. I’m looking for a sponsor. Would you be willing to sponsor me?”

Or:

> “I’m new to AA and would like help working the Steps. Are you available to sponsor someone?”

If you are unsure about making a long-term commitment, ask about starting temporarily:

> “Would you be willing to be my temporary sponsor while I learn more about AA and get started?”

A temporary sponsor can provide an immediate point of contact without suggesting that either person must continue indefinitely. In practice, every sponsorship relationship can be reconsidered if it is not helpful or circumstances change.

Try to ask the actual question rather than hinting and hoping the person volunteers. Clear communication makes it easier for them to give you an honest answer.

## What if they say no?

A potential sponsor may decline because they already sponsor several people, have family or health responsibilities, are traveling, or do not believe they are the right fit. Their answer may have nothing to do with you.

You can respond with:

> “Thank you for being honest. Is there anyone else you think I could speak with?”

Then ask another person. Finding a sponsor sometimes requires several conversations. A refusal is not a rejection by AA, and it does not mean you asked incorrectly.

While searching, continue attending meetings and building a wider support network. Collect phone numbers where that is customary, talk with several members and consider online or phone meetings if local options are limited. A sponsor can be valuable, but one person should not have to provide every kind of support you need.

## Discuss expectations at the beginning

Once someone agrees, arrange an initial conversation about how you will work together. Clarify practical points such as:

- How and when to make contact
- How often you will meet or speak
- Whether you will use particular AA literature
- How Step work will be approached
- What to do if one of you is unavailable
- How each person will raise concerns
- What privacy means to both of you

AA sponsorship does not create the same legally protected confidentiality that may apply to certain healthcare or legal relationships. It is sensible to ask how the sponsor handles sensitive information rather than assuming.

Also discuss urgent situations. A sponsor may provide peer support, but they cannot guarantee constant availability. If you are at immediate risk of harm, contact local emergency services or an appropriate crisis service. For healthcare concerns, speak with a qualified professional.

If you may be physically dependent on alcohol, seek medical guidance before abruptly stopping; withdrawal can be dangerous for some people. AA and sponsorship may complement professional treatment, but they do not replace medical care.

## Know the signs of healthy sponsorship

Healthy sponsorship supports your responsibility and growth rather than demanding obedience. A sponsor may challenge your thinking or suggest actions that feel uncomfortable, such as making calls, attending meetings or writing honestly. However, you retain agency over your life.

Be cautious if a sponsor:

- Demands money, gifts, work or personal favors
- Pressures you toward a romantic or sexual relationship
- Tries to isolate you from family, friends, meetings or professional care
- Claims authority over medication or medical treatment
- Uses threats, humiliation or fear to gain compliance
- Insists that disagreement means you will drink
- Repeatedly violates privacy or stated boundaries
- Discriminates against or belittles you

You can end a sponsorship relationship that feels unsafe, controlling or consistently unhelpful. Speak with other trusted AA members, meeting servants or a qualified professional if you need perspective. Immediate threats, harassment or abuse should be addressed through suitable local safety and legal channels—not treated merely as a sponsorship disagreement.

## How to begin working together

After someone agrees to sponsor you, ask what the next practical action should be. It might be making a call the following day, reading a short section of AA literature, arranging a meeting or discussing the First Step.

Write down agreed contact details and follow through. You do not have to perform perfectly. Honesty, willingness and reasonable consistency matter more than pretending to understand everything.

A sponsor also does not replace the broader AA community. Continue attending meetings, getting to know other members and developing support outside one relationship. Depending on your needs, recovery may also involve counseling, medical care, rehabilitation services, mutual-help alternatives, spiritual communities or support from trusted family and friends.

For ideas about communicating and participating constructively, see these [practical ways to be a good AA sponsee](/blog/how-to-be-a-good-aa-sponsee).

## If you cannot find a sponsor immediately

Do not postpone every part of recovery until you find one person. You can keep attending AA meetings, tell the group you are seeking sponsorship and speak with members after meetings. Some meetings invite people available to sponsor to identify themselves, although local formats differ.

You can also ask a member to help temporarily, attend meetings hosted by different groups or explore online AA meetings. Remote sponsorship may be useful where geography, disability, work schedules, language or limited local meeting options create barriers. Agree on communication methods, time zones and privacy before sharing sensitive information online.

Most importantly, keep taking safe actions that support today’s sobriety. Sponsorship is a relationship that develops through contact and shared work; it does not have to begin perfectly.

## Conclusion

To ask someone to sponsor you, choose a member whose recovery and conduct you respect, speak with them briefly and make a clear request. A simple question—“Would you be willing to sponsor me?”—is enough.

Look for experience, availability, honesty and healthy boundaries rather than perfection. If the person declines or the first arrangement does not work, ask someone else. Finding suitable support is part of the process, not a test you can fail.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should I wait before asking for an AA sponsor?

There is no universal waiting period. You can ask when you feel ready for help understanding AA and working the Steps, including during early sobriety. If you are uncertain, ask someone to sponsor you temporarily while you get to know each other.

### Does my AA sponsor need to have years of sobriety?

AA does not impose a universal minimum length of sobriety for sponsors. More time sober can bring useful experience, but time alone does not guarantee a healthy fit. Look for someone who has worked the Steps, demonstrates stable recovery and understands appropriate boundaries.

### Can I change sponsors?

Yes. Sponsorship is voluntary, and either person can end the relationship. If possible, communicate respectfully and directly: explain that you are grateful for the help but need a different approach. You do not need permission to choose another sponsor, especially if you feel unsafe.

### Can someone sponsor me online or by phone?

Yes, many people communicate with sponsors remotely, particularly when local meetings are unavailable or accessibility is an issue. Discuss contact times, privacy, Step work and backup support. You may also benefit from attending meetings and building connections with several people rather than relying entirely on one remote contact.
