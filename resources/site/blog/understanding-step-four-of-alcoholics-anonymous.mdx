Step Four of Alcoholics Anonymous is an organised and honest look at the patterns, fears, resentments and behaviour that may have affected your life and relationships. It is not a demand to condemn yourself or produce a perfect autobiography. Its purpose is to help you understand yourself more clearly so that change becomes possible.

The wording of Step Four is: “Made a searching and fearless moral inventory of ourselves.” For many newcomers, words such as “fearless”, “moral” and “inventory” can sound intimidating. In practice, the step is usually completed gradually, often with guidance from an AA sponsor or another experienced person in recovery.

This guide explains what the Fourth Step means, what may be included in an inventory and how to approach it without becoming overwhelmed.

## What does Step Four mean in Alcoholics Anonymous?

An inventory is simply a careful review. A business might take stock to discover what it has, what is missing and what needs attention. In Step Four, the subject of that review is your own life.

“Searching” means being thorough enough to notice recurring patterns rather than staying with convenient explanations. “Fearless” does not mean feeling no fear. It can mean proceeding honestly despite discomfort, with suitable support and at a manageable pace.

The word “moral” refers to conduct, values and the effects of your choices. It is not an invitation to label yourself as a good or bad person. The focus is on recognising what happened, your part in it and what you may need to change.

Step Four sits within a wider process. The first three Steps concern recognising a problem, becoming open to help and deciding to follow a new direction. Step Four begins a more structured examination of the attitudes and behaviours that may keep someone stuck. If you want an overview of that sequence, see [the Twelve Steps of Alcoholics Anonymous explained simply](/blog/the-twelve-steps-of-alcoholics-anonymous-explained).

## Why is the Fourth Step important?

Problematic drinking can be accompanied by secrecy, avoidance and blame. Even after alcohol has been removed, familiar reactions may remain. Someone may continue to withdraw when afraid, become defensive when challenged or hold on to resentment long after an event.

A written inventory can bring these patterns into view. It may help you:

- identify resentments that repeatedly disturb you;
- recognise fears influencing your decisions;
- understand where your behaviour has harmed others or yourself;
- notice dishonesty, control, avoidance or self-centredness;
- acknowledge strengths and values you want to develop;
- prepare for the honesty of Step Five.

This is not about taking responsibility for everything that has ever happened. People can be harmed by events for which they were not responsible. Step Four should never be used to excuse abuse, minimise trauma or assign blame to someone who was victimised.

The useful question is often not “Was everything my fault?” but “What is mine to understand, address or change now?” Sometimes the honest answer is that another person caused harm and your present task is to seek support, establish boundaries or stop carrying undeserved shame.

## What goes into a Step Four inventory?

There is no need to invent your own method in isolation. AA literature describes inventory approaches, and sponsors often help newcomers work through them. Individual sponsors may use somewhat different worksheets or formats, so it is sensible to agree on an approach before beginning.

Common areas include the following.

### Resentments

A resentment may be persistent anger, bitterness or a sense of having been wronged. You might write down the person, institution, principle or situation you resent, what happened and which parts of your life felt threatened or affected.

The next stage is to consider your own response. Were you dishonest, controlling, avoidant or unwilling to communicate? Did fear prolong the conflict? This examination does not declare the other party innocent. It asks what you can learn about your own patterns.

### Fears

Fear can operate beneath anger, perfectionism, people-pleasing and attempts to control others. A fear inventory might include fear of rejection, financial insecurity, loneliness, failure, intimacy or losing control.

For each fear, consider how it influences your behaviour. Do you avoid difficult conversations, seek constant reassurance, conceal the truth or make decisions mainly to protect your image? Naming fear can reduce its hidden power and help you discuss a healthier response with someone you trust.

### Relationships and sexual conduct

Some Fourth Step formats examine intimate and other important relationships. The aim is not to judge consensual sexuality or impose another person’s values. It is to look honestly at qualities such as respect, consent, fidelity, manipulation, selfishness, jealousy and responsibility.

Useful questions might include: Was I honest? Did I use another person? Did I ignore someone’s boundaries? What kind of partner or friend do I want to become?

### Harms and recurring behaviour

An inventory may reveal occasions when your choices affected other people. Record these honestly, but do not rush to contact anyone or attempt an immediate apology. Later Steps deal specifically with identifying harms and making amends, including circumstances in which direct contact could cause further harm.

Step Four is about seeing clearly. It is not yet the stage for trying to repair every situation.

### Strengths and positive qualities

Some people also include assets such as courage, kindness, persistence, generosity and willingness. AA Step Four is often associated with difficult behaviour, but a balanced inventory can recognise healthy qualities too.

This is not a way to avoid responsibility. It helps prevent honest reflection from becoming a catalogue of self-attack. Recovery involves building on what is useful as well as changing what is harmful.

## How to begin Step Four practically

Before starting, speak with your sponsor about the format, scope and what you will do if strong emotions arise. In AA, a sponsor is another member who shares their experience of working the Steps. A sponsor is not automatically a therapist, doctor or legal adviser.

The following practical approach may help:

1. **Choose one clear method.** Use the relevant AA material or the format agreed with your sponsor rather than collecting numerous conflicting worksheets.
2. **Set aside manageable periods.** Twenty or thirty focused minutes may be more productive than an exhausting all-day session.
3. **Write plainly.** You do not need polished prose. Brief, specific notes are usually more useful than dramatic explanations.
4. **Focus on patterns.** Repeated reactions often reveal more than an attempt to record every event in your life.
5. **Stay connected.** Continue attending meetings, speaking to supportive people and maintaining the routines that protect your sobriety.
6. **Pause without abandoning the step.** If you become flooded or confused, stop, ground yourself and discuss how to continue safely.

Ordinary recovery routines still matter while doing an inventory. Eating regularly, sleeping as well as you can, attending appointments and using [practical one-day-at-a-time sobriety tools](/blog/how-to-stay-sober-one-day-at-a-time) can keep the work in proportion.

## Common worries about Step Four

### “What if I cannot remember everything?”

A Fourth Step does not have to be a complete historical record. Begin with what you remember and the issues affecting you now. Further insight may emerge later, and ongoing inventory is part of later Step work.

### “What if someone sees what I have written?”

Choose a reasonably private place and store your notes securely. Ask your sponsor how the inventory will be used before sharing it. Avoid leaving sensitive information where it can be casually found.

### “Does fearless mean I must disclose everything immediately?”

No. Courage and care can exist together. Step Four involves writing an inventory; Step Five concerns admitting the nature of your wrongs to a higher power, yourself and another person. Selecting an appropriate listener and setting are important. You can prepare by reading about [honesty, trust and safety in Step Five](/blog/understanding-step-five-of-alcoholics-anonymous).

### “What if the inventory makes me hate myself?”

Self-hatred is not the purpose of Step Four. Try to describe conduct rather than defining your entire identity. “I lied because I was afraid” is more specific and workable than “I am a terrible person.” Accountability can be firm without becoming cruel.

## Step Four, trauma and professional support

An inventory can bring back painful memories. Sponsors can offer valuable peer support and experience of the AA programme, but they are not necessarily trained to treat trauma or mental health conditions.

If writing about abuse, bereavement, self-harm, violence or other traumatic experiences feels destabilising, consider speaking with a qualified mental health professional. You can work the Steps while also receiving clinical care. AA is one source of peer support, not the only route to recovery or a replacement for healthcare.

If you are currently drinking heavily or believe you may be physically dependent on alcohol, seek medical guidance before stopping abruptly. Alcohol withdrawal can be dangerous for some people and may require professional assessment. Step work should not replace appropriate medical support.

## What happens after the inventory?

Step Four leads into Step Five, where the person shares the nature of what they have found with a higher power as they understand it, themselves and another trusted person. This sharing can challenge secrecy and help place the inventory in perspective.

There is no prize for finishing faster than someone else. A useful inventory is honest, sufficiently thorough and completed with willingness. Perfectionism can be another form of avoidance, so agree with your sponsor on what “complete enough” means.

Later Steps address readiness to change, making a list of people harmed, appropriate amends and continuing personal inventory. Step Four is therefore not a one-off attempt to fix your entire life. It is one part of an ongoing practice of honesty and responsibility.

## Conclusion

Step Four of Alcoholics Anonymous is a structured moral inventory designed to uncover patterns that may obstruct recovery. It asks for honesty, not self-punishment; courage, not the absence of fear; and responsibility, not blame for harms done to you.

Take it in manageable stages, use an established format and seek support when needed. The goal is not to produce a perfect document. It is to see yourself more clearly and become willing to move forward.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need a sponsor to complete Step Four?

AA does not operate through professional supervision, but many members find a sponsor’s experience extremely helpful. A sponsor can explain the format, answer practical questions and help you avoid either minimising your behaviour or becoming excessively self-critical. If you do not have a sponsor, you could speak with experienced members at meetings while taking care over whom you trust with sensitive details.

### How long should an AA Fourth Step take?

There is no universal timetable. It may take days, weeks or longer depending on your circumstances and the method being used. Consistent progress is generally more useful than rushing or waiting for perfect confidence. Discuss a realistic pace with your sponsor.

### Is Step Four only a list of bad things I have done?

No. It commonly examines resentments, fears, relationship conduct, harms and personal patterns. Some approaches also identify strengths. The purpose is an honest and useful inventory, not a case for the prosecution.

### Should I make amends while writing my inventory?

Usually, Step Four is for identifying and understanding patterns rather than immediately contacting people. Amends are addressed in Steps Eight and Nine, where willingness, timing, safety and the risk of further harm can be considered carefully. If you feel an urgent need to contact someone, discuss it first with your sponsor or another appropriately qualified adviser.
