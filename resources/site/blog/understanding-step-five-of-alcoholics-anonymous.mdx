Step Five of Alcoholics Anonymous is about sharing the truth you uncovered in Step Four with yourself, a Higher Power and one trusted person. Its purpose is not humiliation or punishment. It is intended to loosen the hold of secrecy, help you recognise harmful patterns and allow another person to offer perspective and support.

The wording of Step Five is: “Admitted to God, to ourselves, and to another human being the exact nature of our wrongs.” For many people, this sounds intimidating. In practice, it is usually a private, carefully prepared conversation rather than a public confession at an AA meeting.

This guide explains what Step Five means, how people commonly approach it and how to choose an appropriate listener. If you are unfamiliar with the wider programme, our guide to [the Twelve Steps of Alcoholics Anonymous explained simply](/blog/the-twelve-steps-of-alcoholics-anonymous-explained) provides useful context.

## What does Step Five mean?

Step Five follows the personal inventory made in Step Four. An inventory is an honest written review of resentments, fears, relationships, conduct and recurring patterns. Step Five involves bringing that review out of isolation and discussing it with another person.

The phrase “exact nature of our wrongs” does not simply mean listing every mistake. It also means looking beneath events to understand patterns such as dishonesty, avoidance, resentment, self-centredness, fear or the need to control situations.

For example, someone may initially focus on an argument with a family member. With careful discussion, they might recognise a wider pattern of hiding feelings until resentment builds, then reacting harshly. That insight can become a practical starting point for change.

Step Five is not about deciding that you are a bad person. It asks you to take responsibility for your conduct without ignoring your humanity, your circumstances or the harm that may also have been done to you.

## Why sharing with another person matters

It is possible to admit something privately while continuing to minimise, justify or misunderstand it. A trusted listener can ask sensible questions, notice repeated themes and help separate responsibility from excessive shame.

Speaking honestly may also challenge the belief that certain experiences must remain hidden at all costs. Many people find that being heard calmly makes their past feel more manageable, even when the facts have not changed.

Potential benefits include:

- reducing the burden of secrecy;
- recognising patterns that were difficult to see alone;
- learning to be honest without exaggerating or minimising;
- accepting appropriate responsibility;
- identifying fears, resentments and habits that need attention;
- developing trust in another person;
- preparing for the willingness and change explored in later Steps.

No particular emotional reaction is required. Some people feel immediate relief, while others feel tired, exposed or emotionally unsettled. Some notice the value of Step Five gradually. None of these responses means the Step has been done incorrectly.

## Who should hear your Step Five?

In AA, many members choose to share Step Five with a sponsor. A sponsor is an AA member who informally supports another member through the Twelve Steps by sharing their own experience. Sponsors are not automatically counsellors, doctors, legal advisers or religious authorities.

A sponsor is not the only possible choice. Depending on your circumstances and beliefs, you might speak with:

- an experienced and trusted AA member;
- a member of the clergy or another spiritual adviser;
- a counsellor, psychotherapist or other suitable professional;
- another mature person who understands the purpose of the Step.

Choose someone who can listen without sensationalising, gossiping or turning the conversation towards themselves. They should be able to remain calm, respect boundaries and help you look honestly at your conduct.

Before beginning, ask directly how privacy will be handled. Do not assume every informal listener has a legal or professional duty of confidentiality. Professionals may also have defined limits to confidentiality, so ask them to explain these in advance.

If your inventory includes abuse, serious trauma, unresolved legal concerns, risk to a child or vulnerable adult, or thoughts of harming yourself or someone else, ordinary peer support may not be enough. Appropriate professional, safeguarding, emergency or legal support may be necessary. Step work should never be used to conceal immediate danger or replace qualified help.

## What does “admitted to God” mean?

Alcoholics Anonymous uses spiritual language, but AA members understand a Higher Power in different ways. Some have a traditional religious faith. Others think in terms of spiritual principles, the AA fellowship, nature, conscience or a source of guidance beyond their own isolated thinking.

For a religious person, admitting wrongs to God may involve prayer. For someone uncertain about belief, it may mean approaching the Step with openness and recognising that complete self-reliance has limitations.

You do not need to copy another person’s beliefs or pretend to have certainty. It is reasonable to discuss concerns about spiritual language with a sponsor or another trusted member. Honest doubt is more useful than forced agreement.

## How to prepare for Step Five

### Review your Step Four inventory

Read through what you have written and organise it clearly. Look for omissions caused by fear, embarrassment or the wish to appear better than you were. Also watch for the opposite tendency: taking responsibility for everything, including conduct that belonged to someone else.

### Speak to your chosen listener beforehand

Agree on a suitable place, likely length and basic boundaries. Pick somewhere private and allow enough time so that neither person feels rushed. Ask what usually happens during a Step Five and whether breaks can be taken.

### Protect your recovery routine

Plan ordinary support around the conversation. Attend meetings if they help you, eat properly, rest and avoid arranging unnecessary pressures immediately afterwards. Keep the telephone numbers of supportive people available.

### Bring clear notes

A structured inventory can make it easier to stay focused. You do not need polished writing. Brief headings or notes are often enough to guide the discussion.

### Expect discomfort without demanding perfection

Courage does not mean having no fear. It means proceeding thoughtfully despite fear. You do not have to tell your story dramatically, remember every detail or produce a particular emotional response.

## What happens during a Step Five conversation?

There is no single script used by every AA member. Commonly, the person reads or talks through their inventory while the listener pays attention, asks occasional questions and helps identify patterns.

The conversation may cover:

- what happened;
- who was affected;
- what your part was;
- where fear, dishonesty, resentment or self-interest appeared;
- what you have avoided acknowledging;
- which patterns continue to affect your life.

A helpful listener should not pressure you to provide graphic details that are irrelevant to understanding the pattern. Equally, Step Five is weakened if important facts are deliberately disguised. The aim is proportionate, meaningful honesty.

Take breaks if needed. If you become overwhelmed, say so. For traumatic experiences, specialist support may be more appropriate than exploring details with an untrained peer. AA support and professional care can be used alongside one another.

## What Step Five is not

Understanding the limits of Step Five can reduce unnecessary fear.

It is not:

- a public announcement at an AA meeting;
- permission for another person to shame or interrogate you;
- an instruction to contact everyone you have harmed;
- a substitute for therapy, medical care, safeguarding or legal guidance;
- a demand to accept blame for harm done to you;
- proof that you must reveal private information to unsafe people.

Step Five is also not the amends stage. Directly approaching people you may have harmed is considered later, in Steps Eight and Nine, and requires careful attention to whether contact could cause further harm. Acting impulsively after Step Five can create avoidable problems.

## Common fears about Step Five

### “The listener will reject me”

Fear of judgement is common. Choosing someone experienced, steady and trustworthy can help. You may wish to discuss this fear before arranging the full conversation.

### “My past is worse than everyone else’s”

Shame often creates a sense of being uniquely unacceptable. A suitable listener will not compare stories competitively or dismiss their seriousness. Their role is to help you face the truth constructively.

### “I will leave something out”

Step work does not require perfect memory. Be as honest and thorough as you reasonably can. If something important comes to mind later, it can be discussed then.

### “Admitting a wrong means excusing what others did”

It does not. You can acknowledge your behaviour while also recognising that another person harmed you. Responsibility is not the same as accepting blame for everything.

## What comes after Step Five?

After completing Step Five, some AA members spend quiet time reflecting on the experience and reviewing what they have learnt. Avoid making sudden disclosures, confrontations or amends simply because strong emotions have surfaced.

The next part of the programme turns towards readiness to let go of unhelpful patterns. Our guide to [Step Six and willingness to change](/blog/understanding-step-six-of-alcoholics-anonymous) explains that transition.

The honesty practised here also becomes useful later. Step Ten develops the habit of noticing mistakes and addressing them promptly rather than allowing secrecy and resentment to accumulate. You can explore this in our guide to [daily inventory and prompt honesty in Step Ten](/blog/understanding-step-ten-of-alcoholics-anonymous).

Remember that AA is one route to recovery, not the only one. Some people also use counselling, addiction services, peer-support alternatives, medication prescribed by a clinician or other forms of community support.

If you are drinking heavily or think you may be physically dependent on alcohol, seek medical guidance before stopping suddenly. Abrupt withdrawal can be dangerous for some people, and Step work is not a substitute for medical assessment or supervised withdrawal support.

## Conclusion

Step Five of Alcoholics Anonymous turns private reflection into shared honesty. By discussing a Step Four inventory with a carefully chosen person, you can begin to recognise patterns, reduce secrecy and prepare for practical change.

It does not require public disclosure, perfect recall or self-punishment. The aim is honest responsibility held within dignity, sensible boundaries and appropriate support.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I have to complete Step Five with an AA sponsor?

No. Many AA members choose a sponsor because that person understands the Steps from experience, but others use a suitable spiritual adviser, counsellor or trusted individual. The important considerations are trust, maturity, privacy and an understanding of the Step’s purpose.

### Is Step Five the same as making amends?

No. Step Five involves admitting the nature of your wrongs to yourself, a Higher Power and another person. Amends are addressed later in Steps Eight and Nine. Do not contact people impulsively while completing Step Five.

### Must I share every private detail?

The Step calls for meaningful honesty about your wrongs and underlying patterns. This does not necessarily require graphic or irrelevant detail. Discuss concerns with your chosen listener beforehand, especially where trauma, safety, confidentiality or legal issues are involved.

### What if I remember something after completing Step Five?

You can discuss it later with your sponsor or chosen listener. Recovery is an ongoing process, and no inventory depends on perfect memory. What matters is a continuing willingness to be honest when new understanding emerges.
