Sponsoring someone through the Twelve Steps means sharing how you worked the Steps, helping another AA member apply them honestly, and encouraging them to rely on the wider recovery fellowship—not controlling their choices or acting as a therapist.

A good Alcoholics Anonymous sponsor offers experience, consistency and a safe place for honest conversation. You do not need perfect sobriety, professional expertise or an answer to every problem. You do need enough experience with the Steps to explain what helped you, the humility to admit what you do not know, and the willingness to maintain clear boundaries.

Sponsorship is an informal, voluntary relationship within AA. Sponsors are not certified by Alcoholics Anonymous, and approaches vary among individuals, groups and countries. The suggestions below are practical guidance rather than official AA rules.

## What does an AA sponsor actually do?

An AA sponsor is usually a member who has made progress through the Twelve Steps and helps another member—often called a sponsee—work through them. The relationship is based on one person sharing their experience, strength and hope with another.

Typical responsibilities include:

- Explaining each Step in clear, everyday language
- Suggesting relevant AA literature and practical written work
- Listening without unnecessary judgement
- Helping the sponsee connect the Steps with daily behavior
- Encouraging regular AA meeting attendance and wider fellowship support
- Sharing personal experience without presenting it as the only way
- Recognizing when professional help is needed

A sponsor is not a doctor, counselor, financial adviser, spiritual authority or substitute for every other relationship. Sponsorship can support recovery, but AA is not the only route to sobriety. Healthcare, therapy, medication, rehabilitation programs, secular recovery groups and other community resources may also be valuable.

## Agree on expectations at the beginning

A short, honest conversation can prevent confusion later. Discuss how often you will speak, when you are generally available and what each of you expects from the relationship.

Useful questions include:

- Are we both willing to meet or speak regularly?
- Will we work from particular AA literature or use written exercises?
- What should the sponsee do if I cannot answer the phone?
- How will we handle missed calls or meetings?
- Are there topics that require professional support?
- How will either person end or change the arrangement respectfully?

Avoid promising constant availability. Instead, help the person develop a support network that might include several AA contacts, meetings, healthcare professionals and trusted people outside AA.

Privacy is important, but do not make absolute promises that could conflict with urgent safety needs or local law. If someone appears to be in immediate danger, may harm themselves or another person, or has a medical emergency, contact local emergency services or seek qualified professional help.

## Sponsor from experience rather than authority

Effective sponsorship sounds like, “This is what I did and what happened,” rather than, “You must do exactly what I say.”

AA members sometimes have firm views about meeting frequency, prayer, relationships, medication or how quickly the Steps should be completed. A sponsor can make clear suggestions, but should not use fear, shame or threats to secure compliance.

In particular, do not tell a sponsee to stop prescribed medication, reject therapy or ignore medical advice. Decisions about medication and treatment belong with the individual and an appropriately qualified healthcare professional.

Abruptly stopping alcohol can also be dangerous for some people who have developed physical dependence. If a sponsee reports withdrawal symptoms, previous severe withdrawal, seizures, hallucinations or serious illness, encourage prompt medical assessment rather than offering detoxification instructions.

## How to guide someone through the Twelve Steps

Before starting, make sure you understand the overall sequence. This [simple explanation of the Twelve Steps](/blog/the-twelve-steps-of-alcoholics-anonymous-explained) can help clarify the purpose of each one.

### Steps One to Three: acceptance, hope and a new direction

In Step One, help the sponsee examine what happened when they drank and how alcohol affected their choices, relationships, health or responsibilities. Avoid diagnosing them. Your role is to ask honest questions and share how you recognized powerlessness and unmanageability in your own life.

In Step Two, discuss the possibility that help beyond individual willpower can restore hope and clearer thinking. A “Higher Power” is understood differently across AA. Do not force a religious interpretation. A sponsee may think of the AA group, the recovery process, spiritual principles, God as they understand God, or another source of support greater than themselves.

Step Three is about making a decision to stop directing life entirely through fear, self-will or isolation. Help translate that decision into action: attending a meeting, calling before drinking, beginning written inventory work or following appropriate professional advice.

### Steps Four and Five: inventory and honest sharing

Step Four asks for a searching and fearless moral inventory. Explain that this is not an exercise in self-punishment. It is a structured look at resentments, fears, harms, relationship patterns and the person’s own behavior.

Offer a clear format and manageable deadlines. Breaking the work into short sessions can reduce avoidance. Our [practical guide to Step Four](/blog/understanding-step-four-of-alcoholics-anonymous) explores how to approach inventory with honesty and care.

Be alert to your limits. Inventory work may bring up trauma, abuse, violence or serious mental health concerns. A sponsor should not investigate traumatic memories or attempt to provide therapy. Encourage support from a qualified trauma-informed professional where appropriate.

In Step Five, the person admits the nature of their wrongs to themselves, their Higher Power and another human being. A sponsor often acts as that listener, but the sponsee may choose someone else. Listen calmly, avoid dramatic reactions and do not turn the conversation into a comparison with your own life.

### Steps Six and Seven: willingness and humility

Steps Six and Seven concern becoming ready to change harmful patterns and humbly seeking help to do so. Help the sponsee move beyond labels such as “selfish” or “dishonest” by identifying behavior that can be practiced differently.

For example, willingness might mean pausing before reacting, telling the truth promptly, keeping commitments or asking for help. Humility does not mean humiliation or low self-worth. It means seeing strengths and limitations accurately while remaining teachable.

Sponsors can support this stage by asking, “What would the opposite behavior look like today?” Keep the focus on repeated practice rather than instant personality change.

### Steps Eight and Nine: responsibility and safe amends

Step Eight involves listing people who were harmed and becoming willing to make amends. Do not rush the person into contact. Help them distinguish genuine harm from guilt, shame or responsibility for what other people did.

Step Nine involves direct amends where appropriate, except when doing so would injure the person or others. This safety condition is essential. An amend is not simply an apology; it may involve acknowledging specific behavior, asking how to repair damage and changing future conduct.

Never pressure someone to contact a person who abused them, enter an unsafe environment, disclose information that could harm another person, or take action without appropriate legal or clinical guidance. Financial, legal, workplace and family situations may require advice from a qualified professional. Use this [guide to making Step Nine amends safely](/blog/understanding-step-nine-of-alcoholics-anonymous) when discussing preparation and possible risks.

### Steps Ten and Eleven: daily maintenance

Step Ten brings inventory into everyday life. Help the sponsee establish a brief routine for noticing resentment, fear, dishonesty and harm, while also recognizing gratitude and constructive action. When they are wrong, encourage prompt responsibility—not endless self-criticism.

Step Eleven develops conscious contact with a Higher Power through prayer, meditation or reflective practice. Respect the sponsee’s beliefs. Practical options may include quiet reflection, mindful breathing, reading a short spiritual passage, writing a gratitude list or asking for guidance before a difficult conversation.

These Steps should support responsible action, not replace it. Reflection can help someone become calmer, but they may still need to apologize, attend an appointment or ask for assistance.

### Step Twelve: service and carrying the message

Step Twelve brings together spiritual growth, helping others and practicing recovery principles in daily life. Discuss service opportunities that fit the person’s circumstances, such as welcoming newcomers, helping prepare a meeting space or taking a group responsibility.

Sponsoring others may eventually become part of their service, but it should not be treated as proof of status. Encourage them to share what they have genuinely experienced and to continue working on their own recovery.

## Keep healthy sponsorship boundaries

Boundaries protect both people and make honest support more sustainable. A sponsor should not control a sponsee’s relationships, money, employment, housing, healthcare or personal beliefs.

Avoid lending or borrowing significant sums, entering business arrangements that create pressure, or developing romantic or sexual involvement within the sponsorship relationship. If attraction, dependency or another conflict makes the relationship unsafe or confusing, discuss ending the arrangement and help the person find another sponsor.

Also watch for overdependence. Your aim is not to become indispensable. Encourage the sponsee to build confidence, use meetings, speak with other members and make their own responsible decisions.

## What to do when a sponsee is struggling

A return to drinking, missed calls or reluctance to complete Step work does not justify humiliation. Speak directly but compassionately. Ask what happened, listen for barriers and discuss the next useful action.

You can remain supportive without taking responsibility for another adult’s sobriety. If the relationship repeatedly becomes dishonest, abusive or unworkable, it is acceptable to set limits or end sponsorship respectfully. Where possible, encourage the person to stay connected with AA and find another suitable sponsor.

## Conclusion

To sponsor someone through the Twelve Steps, be available, honest and consistent while staying within the limits of peer support. Explain the Steps from your own experience, encourage practical action, respect different beliefs and place safety ahead of rigid interpretations.

You are not expected to rescue or manage another person. Good sponsorship helps someone build a broader foundation for recovery and gradually learn to practice the Steps for themselves.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need to complete all Twelve Steps before sponsoring someone?

There is no sponsor certification process in AA. Many members recommend having worked the Steps with a sponsor before guiding someone else. If you have not completed a particular Step, be honest about that and seek guidance from your own sponsor or another experienced member.

### How quickly should I take a sponsee through the Steps?

AA does not prescribe one universal timetable. Some people benefit from steady early progress, while others need more time because of health, literacy, trauma or life circumstances. Avoid both unnecessary delay and unsafe pressure. Agree on regular, manageable actions.

### Can I sponsor someone online or by phone?

Yes. Some sponsorship relationships operate partly or entirely through phone calls or video meetings, particularly when distance, disability, work or limited local meetings create barriers. Agree on privacy, timing and backup support if communication fails.

### What if my sponsee does not believe in God?

Do not force a religious belief. AA allows members to develop their own understanding of a Higher Power. Explore language and practices the person can approach honestly, such as relying on the group, recovery principles, shared experience or a sense of purpose beyond individual willpower.
