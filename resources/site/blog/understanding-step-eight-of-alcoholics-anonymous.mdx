Step Eight of Alcoholics Anonymous is about identifying the people harmed by our behaviour and becoming willing to put things right. It says: “Made a list of all persons we had harmed, and became willing to make amends to them all.”

This Step is preparation rather than action. You are not being asked to contact everyone immediately, deliver apologies or solve every past problem. The task is to look honestly at harm, write a careful list and develop willingness for the amends work that may follow in Step Nine.

That distinction matters. A thoughtful Step Eight can help prevent rushed apologies, unsafe contact and attempts to gain forgiveness before considering what the other person actually needs.

## What does Step Eight mean?

Step Eight has two connected parts:

1. **Making a list of people you have harmed.**
2. **Becoming willing to make amends to them.**

In this context, an “amend” means more than saying sorry. It involves taking responsibility and, where appropriate, repairing damage or changing your behaviour. The exact form is considered later. During Step Eight, the focus remains on honest recognition and willingness.

“Willingness” does not mean feeling confident, eager or free from fear. It may simply mean being open to guidance and no longer refusing to consider putting something right.

For an overview of where this work sits within the wider programme, see [the Twelve Steps of Alcoholics Anonymous explained simply](/blog/the-twelve-steps-of-alcoholics-anonymous-explained).

## How Step Eight follows the earlier Steps

The earlier Steps prepare a person for this inventory. In Step Four, AA members examine patterns, fears, resentments and conduct. Step Five involves sharing that inventory with another person, often a sponsor.

A sponsor is an experienced AA member who voluntarily supports another member through the programme. A sponsor is not a therapist, doctor or legal adviser, but they may offer practical experience of how they worked the Steps.

Steps Six and Seven focus on becoming ready to change unhelpful patterns. Step Eight then turns attention towards the effects those patterns had on other people.

The aim is not endless guilt. It is to move from vague shame towards specific responsibility. “I am a terrible person” gives little direction. “I repeatedly broke promises and left someone to deal with the consequences” identifies conduct that can be addressed.

## What counts as harm in Step Eight?

Harm is not limited to dramatic events. It may be emotional, financial, practical, relational or physical. It can include things done directly as well as responsibilities repeatedly neglected.

Examples might include:

- dishonesty, manipulation or broken promises;
- borrowing or taking money without repaying it;
- neglecting children, partners, relatives or friends;
- failing to turn up for work or leaving colleagues to cover responsibilities;
- aggressive, threatening or humiliating behaviour;
- damaging property or creating financial problems;
- infidelity or breaches of trust;
- driving or behaving in ways that put others at risk;
- withdrawing affection or using silence to punish someone;
- involving others in secrecy or repeatedly causing worry.

The list is about the impact of your own conduct. It is not a list of people you dislike, people who have hurt you or people who “owe” you an apology.

It is also important not to take responsibility for being abused, exploited or mistreated. If a relationship involved trauma, coercion or violence, work slowly with appropriate support. Recognising your own conduct never requires accepting blame for another person’s abuse.

## How to work Step Eight practically

There is no need to produce a perfect list in one sitting. Step Eight can be approached calmly and methodically.

### Start with your earlier inventory

Review any relevant notes from Step Four and discussions from Step Five. Look for names connected with dishonesty, fear, resentment, relationships, money or neglected responsibilities.

Some people organise their list by areas of life, such as:

- family and intimate relationships;
- friends and neighbours;
- work, education or volunteering;
- finances and property;
- people affected by dangerous or irresponsible behaviour;
- organisations or communities harmed by their conduct.

Keep the list private and stored securely. It may contain sensitive information about you and other people.

### Write down the harm, not the whole argument

Beside each name, briefly describe what you did and the likely effect. Try to avoid turning the entry into a case for the defence.

For example, instead of writing, “I lied because they were always criticising me,” you might write, “I repeatedly lied about money, which damaged trust and left them dealing with unpaid bills.”

Context may matter later, but Step Eight is not primarily about proving who was right. It is about seeing your part clearly.

### Avoid deciding the amend too soon

You might immediately imagine a face-to-face conversation, but that may not be suitable. Some amends involve repayment, returning property, correcting information or demonstrating dependable behaviour over time. In other situations, direct contact could cause distress or create danger.

For now, list the person and the harm. The form, timing and safety of any amend belong to careful Step Nine planning.

### Discuss the list with a sponsor or trusted support

A sponsor can help identify minimising, exaggeration or overlooked patterns. They may also help separate a genuine amend from an attempt to relieve guilt at someone else’s expense.

If the situation includes abuse, serious mental health concerns, safeguarding issues, legal consequences or complicated debt, support from a qualified professional may also be appropriate. AA experience does not replace legal, medical, financial or therapeutic guidance.

### Work on willingness honestly

Some names may bring immediate willingness. Others may trigger anger, fear or shame. That does not mean you have failed the Step.

Helpful ways to build willingness include:

- discussing resistance honestly with a sponsor;
- considering what the other person experienced;
- remembering that willingness is not the same as immediate contact;
- using prayer or quiet reflection, if that is meaningful to you;
- focusing on responsibility rather than whether forgiveness is offered;
- allowing the list to develop instead of forcing instant certainty.

AA uses spiritual language, but members understand it in different ways. A person can approach willingness through prayer, reflection, values, conscience or a commitment to stop causing further harm.

## Common fears about Step Eight

Step Eight can bring up the fear that every past mistake must be exposed at once. That is not what the Step asks.

You may worry that someone will reject you, that consequences will follow or that an apology will reopen an old wound. These concerns are reasons to seek guidance, not reasons to make impulsive contact.

Another common difficulty is shame. Shame may encourage avoidance, but it can also lead to overreaction—such as making sweeping confessions or placing yourself in unsafe situations. Responsible amends are measured by whether they repair harm where possible, not by how much discomfort you can endure.

The list also does not depend on remembering every person immediately. Recovery memories can emerge gradually. You can add a name later without treating the original list as a failure.

## Step Eight is not Step Nine

Step Eight creates the list and willingness. Step Nine is where direct amends may be made, except when doing so would injure the person concerned or someone else.

Before contacting anyone, consider the likely effect, your motives and whether specialist advice is needed. An amend should not be used to demand forgiveness, restart an unwanted relationship, disclose information that harms an uninvolved person or pressure someone to respond.

Our guide to [making amends safely in Step Nine](/blog/understanding-step-nine-of-alcoholics-anonymous) explains how to think about timing, boundaries and different forms of restitution.

## What happens after Step Eight?

Completing Step Eight does not mean every relationship will be restored. Some people may not accept an amend, and some damage cannot be fully repaired. The recovering person can still take responsibility, respect boundaries and live differently.

Later, Step Ten encourages a regular personal inventory so that new mistakes can be addressed more promptly. This helps prevent another long backlog of unresolved harm. Learn more about [daily inventory and prompt honesty in Step Ten](/blog/understanding-step-ten-of-alcoholics-anonymous).

## Conclusion

Step Eight of Alcoholics Anonymous is an honest but measured preparation for change. It asks you to identify whom you harmed, understand your part and become willing to put things right without rushing into unsafe or self-serving contact.

Approached with support, privacy and patience, the Step can turn shame into responsibility and prepare the ground for thoughtful amends and more dependable relationships.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I contact people while working Step Eight?

Usually, no. Step Eight is about making the list and developing willingness. Contact and restitution are considered in Step Nine, ideally after discussing safety, motives and timing with a sponsor or other appropriate adviser.

### What if I cannot remember everyone I harmed?

Begin with the people and situations you can remember. Review earlier inventory work and look across family, friendships, work and finances. The list can be updated if further memories become clear.

### Should I include myself on the Step Eight list?

Some AA members and sponsors include themselves, while others keep the Step focused on harm to other people. Speak with your sponsor about their approach. Self-compassion and healthier behaviour matter, but including yourself should not become a way to avoid responsibility to others.

### Can I work Step Eight without a sponsor?

It is possible to write a list independently, but trusted guidance can be valuable. A sponsor may notice excuses, excessive self-blame or unsafe plans that are difficult to recognise alone. Professional support is also worth considering where trauma, safeguarding, legal or serious financial issues are involved.
