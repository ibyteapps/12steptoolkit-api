Step Three of Alcoholics Anonymous is about making a decision: instead of trying to manage recovery and life entirely through self-will, you become willing to trust guidance beyond your own immediate impulses. In AA, this is expressed as turning your will and life over to the care of God “as we understood Him”.

For newcomers, that wording can feel comforting, confusing or even alienating. You do not need a settled religious belief or a perfect understanding of a Higher Power to begin. Step Three is commonly approached as a decision to stop fighting everything alone, accept help and take recovery-guided action.

It is not a demand for passivity, blind obedience or the loss of personal responsibility. The decision becomes meaningful through what you do next: speaking honestly, accepting support, attending meetings and continuing with the remaining Steps.

## What is Step Three of Alcoholics Anonymous?

Step Three states:

> “Made a decision to turn our will and our lives over to the care of God as we understood Him.”

This follows naturally from the first two Steps. Step One involves recognising a lack of control over alcohol and the unmanageability connected with it. Step Two introduces the possibility that help beyond isolated self-reliance can restore clearer thinking. Step Three is the decision to rely on that help.

If you are unfamiliar with the wider programme, this [simple guide to the Twelve Steps of Alcoholics Anonymous](/blog/the-twelve-steps-of-alcoholics-anonymous-explained) explains how the Steps connect.

Different AA members understand Step Three differently. Some have a traditional faith. Some use “Higher Power” to mean the collective experience of their AA group, the principles of recovery, nature, love or a spiritual reality they cannot define. Others remain agnostic while practising willingness, honesty and openness.

AA is a spiritual rather than a formally religious fellowship. Individual meetings and members may express spirituality in different ways, but no newcomer needs to pretend to hold beliefs they do not have.

## What does “turning over your will” mean?

In everyday language, “will” can mean your intentions, choices and determination. In Step Three, the phrase often points towards loosening the grip of self-will: the assumption that you must control every person, result and feeling to be safe.

Turning your will over does not mean becoming unable to make decisions. It can mean pausing before acting and asking:

- Is this choice honest?
- Does it protect my sobriety?
- Am I acting from fear, resentment or pride?
- Have I asked for help from someone trustworthy?
- What would patience, courage or kindness look like here?

You still take action. The difference is that your actions are guided by recovery principles rather than every passing urge.

For example, you cannot control whether someone forgives you, but you can behave honestly. You cannot guarantee that tomorrow will be easy, but you can take sensible action today. This focus fits closely with the AA practice of [staying sober one day at a time](/blog/how-to-stay-sober-one-day-at-a-time).

## Step Three is a decision, not a feeling

The wording of Step Three is important: it asks you to make a decision. It does not say that you must feel completely peaceful, certain or spiritually transformed.

You might make the decision while still feeling doubtful. Trust can develop gradually as you attend meetings, work with a sponsor and notice the results of taking healthier actions.

A sponsor is an AA member who informally guides another member through the Steps by sharing their own experience. Sponsors are not therapists, doctors or authority figures, and arrangements vary. A helpful sponsor supports your thinking rather than controlling your life.

The decision in Step Three is sometimes compared to deciding to begin a journey. Choosing the destination matters, but the decision alone does not take you there. The following Steps provide concrete action, beginning with the personal inventory of Step Four. Our [practical guide to Step Four](/blog/understanding-step-four-of-alcoholics-anonymous) explains how that honest self-review can be approached with care.

## Does Step Three require belief in God?

Step Three uses the word “God”, but the qualifying phrase “as we understood Him” allows room for personal understanding. Many members also use the term “Higher Power”.

If religious language is difficult because of past experiences, uncertainty or firm personal beliefs, you can be honest about that. Forced belief is unlikely to produce meaningful trust. It may be more useful to begin with a simple question: are you willing to accept that your own thinking does not have to be your only source of guidance?

A newcomer might initially place trust in:

- The shared experience of people who have stayed sober
- The AA group as a source of collective support
- Recovery principles such as honesty and service
- A spiritual tradition they already value
- An undefined source of care, meaning or wisdom

Your understanding can change over time. You do not need to settle every spiritual question before taking the next helpful action.

## What surrender does—and does not—mean

“Surrender” is another word often heard in AA. In this context, it generally means ending an exhausting fight against the reality that alcohol has become a serious problem and accepting appropriate help.

It does not mean:

- Giving up on yourself
- Letting other people make every decision
- Staying in an unsafe relationship or situation
- Ignoring medical, legal or professional advice
- Assuming every event is meant to happen
- Avoiding responsibility for harm caused

Healthy surrender includes boundaries and practical judgement. If a person claims that spirituality requires you to tolerate abuse, stop medication or disregard professional care, seek advice from an appropriate qualified professional and trusted sources of support.

AA can be valuable, but it is not the only route to recovery. Some people also use counselling, NHS or private alcohol services, medication prescribed by a clinician, residential treatment, secular peer support or combinations of these.

## How to practise Step Three

Step Three can seem abstract until it is connected to ordinary behaviour. The following practices may help.

### Talk it through with someone trustworthy

Speak to a sponsor or an experienced AA member about how they approached the Step. Ask what “turning it over” looks like in their daily life, rather than searching for a perfect definition.

You can listen to several perspectives. Another person’s understanding does not have to become yours.

### Write down what you are trying to control

Make a short list of situations occupying your thoughts. Beside each one, separate what is within your control from what is not.

You may be unable to control another person’s response, for example, but able to control whether you communicate respectfully. This exercise turns surrender into a practical distinction between responsibility and control.

### Pause before important actions

When possible, avoid acting immediately on anger, fear or craving. Take a few slow breaths, contact a supportive person or wait until your thinking is clearer.

A pause is not avoidance. It creates room for a choice that supports recovery.

### Use prayer or quiet reflection

Some AA members mark Step Three with a prayer. You may use wording from AA literature, your own faith tradition or simple personal words. If prayer does not fit your beliefs, try quiet reflection or meditation.

A simple intention might be: “Help me recognise the next honest and useful action, and give me the willingness to take it.” The purpose is not to produce special feelings. It is to shift attention away from panic and towards constructive action.

### Follow the decision with action

Attend a meeting, call your sponsor, keep an appointment or begin Step Four with support. Step Three is strengthened by repeated behaviour.

Small actions count. Making a meal, resting, apologising promptly or declining an invitation that threatens your sobriety can all reflect care for your recovery.

## Common difficulties with Step Three

### “I keep taking my will back”

Many people make a Step Three decision and then return to controlling or impulsive habits. This does not necessarily mean the Step has failed. Recovery involves repetition.

When you notice old patterns, pause and make the decision again. Step Three can become a daily practice rather than a single dramatic event.

### “I do not trust anyone or anything”

Trust may be difficult after disappointment, trauma or repeated broken promises. Start carefully. You might trust a sensible recovery action before you trust a spiritual idea—for example, attending a meeting instead of drinking or asking for help before making a major decision.

Professional therapeutic support may also be useful where trust is connected with painful experiences. AA members can share experience, but they are not a substitute for qualified mental health care.

### “Does this mean I should wait for an answer?”

Step Three is not usually about waiting for a supernatural instruction. Guidance may appear through careful reflection, sound advice, recovery principles and an honest assessment of likely consequences.

For serious medical, financial, legal or safeguarding matters, consult an appropriately qualified professional. A sponsor can support your recovery thinking but should not replace specialist advice.

## A note about stopping alcohol safely

If you drink heavily or regularly and may be physically dependent, abruptly stopping alcohol can be dangerous. Withdrawal can require medical assessment and treatment. Speak to a GP, NHS alcohol service or another qualified healthcare professional for guidance rather than attempting to manage possible withdrawal alone. Call emergency services if severe symptoms occur.

Step Three can support the willingness to seek help, but it is not a medical treatment or a substitute for safe clinical care.

## Conclusion

Step Three of Alcoholics Anonymous is a decision to move away from isolated self-will and towards trust, support and principled action. You do not need perfect faith, complete certainty or a particular emotional experience.

Begin with willingness. Ask for help, pause before acting and choose the next step that protects your recovery. Over time, repeated actions can give the decision a deeper and more personal meaning.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can an atheist or agnostic complete AA Step Three?

Yes. Many atheist and agnostic AA members interpret a Higher Power in non-religious ways, such as the group, collective experience or recovery principles. You can remain honest about your beliefs while becoming willing to accept help beyond your own isolated thinking.

### Do I need a sponsor before taking Step Three?

There is no requirement to have a sponsor before considering Step Three, but guidance from an experienced and trustworthy AA member can be helpful. A sponsor can explain how they worked the Step and help you connect the decision with practical action.

### Is Step Three completed only once?

It may be formally worked once as part of progressing through the Steps, but many AA members revisit its principle regularly. Letting go of control and choosing recovery-guided action can become a daily practice.

### What should I do after Step Three?

In the Twelve Step sequence, Step Four comes next. It involves making a searching and honest personal inventory. Work at a steady pace, discuss concerns with a sponsor or trusted guide, and seek professional support if difficult experiences arise that need specialist care.
